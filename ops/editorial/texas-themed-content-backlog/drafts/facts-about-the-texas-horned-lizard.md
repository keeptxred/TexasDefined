# 12 Facts About the Texas Horned Lizard — the “Horny Toad”

Few Texas animals trigger nostalgia like the horned lizard. Generations of Texans remember seeing “horny toads” in yards, school grounds and dusty lots, while many younger residents have never seen one in the wild.

That contrast is part of the story. The Texas horned lizard is still present across parts of the state, but it is listed as threatened in Texas and has declined from many places where it was once common.

## 1. It is a lizard, not a toad

The nickname “horny toad” comes from the animal’s squat, flattened body and spiny appearance. Biologically it is a reptile: *Phrynosoma cornutum*, the Texas horned lizard.

## 2. Its head horns are real extensions of the skull

The animal’s dramatic head spines are not soft decorative scales. They help create the armored silhouette that makes the species instantly recognizable.

TPWD notes two especially prominent central head spines among the features used to identify the species.

## 3. Camouflage is one of its best defenses

Brown, tan and mottled coloration helps horned lizards disappear against dry soil, gravel and sparse vegetation.

Their first defense is often to stay still and rely on camouflage rather than sprint away.

## 4. They prefer open, dry habitat

Texas Parks and Wildlife describes the species as using arid and semiarid habitat with sparse vegetation, often where loose sand or loamy soils allow digging.

They burrow for nesting, hibernation and protection from temperature extremes.

## 5. Harvester ants are a major food source

Texas horned lizards are strongly associated with harvester ants. TPWD’s long-running Horned Lizard Watch data found a positive relationship between lizard observations and the presence of harvester ants.

They also eat other insects, but ant-rich native habitat is an important part of the conservation story.

## 6. Fire ants can be part of the problem, but the decline is bigger than one species

Red imported fire ants can compete with native ants and affect ecosystems, but horned-lizard decline should not be reduced to one villain.

Habitat loss and fragmentation, pesticide use, changes in native ant communities, roads and other pressures can interact. Use current TPWD research rather than repeating a one-cause explanation.

## 7. Texas lists the species as threatened

TPWD currently lists the Texas horned lizard as threatened in the state.

That legal status means people should not collect wild horned lizards as pets. Current possession/handling rules should be verified immediately before publication.

## 8. They still occur across broad parts of Texas

Decline does not mean extinction. TPWD describes the species as ranging through much of Texas and into neighboring states and northern Mexico, with stronger populations remaining in some South, West and Northwest Texas regions.

Avoid statements such as “they have disappeared from Texas.” The accurate story is regional decline and uneven persistence.

## 9. They spend part of the year underground

Horned lizards use soil for hibernation and insulation. Seasonal inactivity means absence in winter does not prove a local population is gone.

This also explains why soil disturbance and habitat structure matter as much as visible vegetation.

## 10. Their defensive behavior is stranger than their appearance

Horned lizards can puff themselves up, flatten against the ground and use their spines defensively. Some horned-lizard species, including Texas horned lizards, are famous for the ability to squirt blood from around the eyes under severe threat.

That behavior is a last-resort defense, not something visitors should provoke for a video.

## 11. Citizen science helped Texans track the species

TPWD’s Texas Horned Lizard Watch engaged volunteers in reporting sightings and habitat information over many years.

That project is useful for TexasDefined because it shows how ordinary residents can contribute observations without collecting wildlife.

## 12. A horned-lizard-friendly yard looks less manicured than a golf course

Native plants, open patches of soil, leaf litter and healthy native insect communities can create better habitat than an intensively treated lawn.

Any homeowner recommendations should be grounded in TPWD or Texas A&M AgriLife guidance and should not encourage people to introduce ants or relocate wild lizards.

## What to do if you see one

- Observe from a respectful distance.
- Do not take it home.
- Keep pets from harassing it if possible.
- Avoid unnecessary pesticide use around known habitat.
- Photograph without handling when practical.
- Use current TPWD citizen-science/reporting guidance if a reporting program is active.
