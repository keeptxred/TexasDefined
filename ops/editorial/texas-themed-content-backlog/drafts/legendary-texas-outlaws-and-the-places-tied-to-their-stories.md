# Legendary Texas Outlaws and the Places Tied to Their Stories

Outlaw stories are unusually vulnerable to folklore. This guide should separate documented crimes, arrests and movements from later tourism legends about caves, hideouts and secret tunnels.

## Figures to research

### Sam Bass
His train-robbery career, Denton-area associations and death after the Round Rock gunfight are well documented, but specific “hideout” claims need independent evidence.

### John Wesley Hardin
A violent gunman whose own autobiography helped shape his legend. Claims based only on his memoir should be labeled as self-reported.

### Bonnie Parker and Clyde Barrow
North Texas and Dallas-area connections are central to their story, but the couple operated across several states.

### Belle Starr
Her Texas childhood and later outlaw associations became heavily romanticized; distinguish documented events from dime-novel reputation.

## Avoid glamorizing violence

The article should identify victims and legal consequences where relevant and avoid framing murder, robbery or intimidation as harmless adventure.

## Responsible place-based storytelling

Only direct readers to museums, markers, public roads, archives and lawful historic sites. Never publish directions to private “hideouts,” caves, abandoned buildings or other trespass-prone locations.

## Internal-link plan

Link to Texas history, ghost towns, historic downtowns, Ranger history and county pages tied to documented events.

## Source notes

Use court records, TSLAC, Handbook of Texas entries, local historical commissions and contemporary newspapers where accessible. Treat uncorroborated tourism lore as folklore, not fact.

## Image guidance

Use rights-cleared archival portraits, wanted posters or historic-town images with source provenance. Avoid sensational crime-scene imagery.
