# Signs You Know You’re From Texas

This should read as affectionate cultural observation, not a purity test for who is a “real Texan.” Texas is too large and diverse for one checklist to fit everyone.

## Possible signs

1. You give driving distance in hours more naturally than miles.
2. You understand that “the Hill Country,” “the Valley,” “the Panhandle” and “East Texas” describe very different worlds.
3. You know a county courthouse square can be the center of a road trip.
4. You have a personal opinion about barbecue style but know Texas barbecue is not one recipe.
5. You understand why spring wildflowers can change weekend traffic patterns.
6. You have experienced a weather forecast that went from heat to hail faster than expected.
7. You know high-school football can involve bands, drill teams, families and entire communities—not only players.
8. You recognize that “y’all” is both efficient and inclusive when used naturally.
9. You can name at least one road trip that requires a fuel stop before the scenery changes.
10. You know Gulf Coast, West Texas, South Texas and Piney Woods food traditions are not interchangeable.
11. You have seen a blue north or sudden temperature drop change the day’s plan.
12. You know a small town may be famous for one festival, courthouse, food or historic site.
13. You have strong feelings about a Texas grocery, convenience-store or fast-food institution—but not necessarily the same one as your neighbor.
14. You understand that Texas identity includes Indigenous, Mexican, Tejano, Black, German, Czech, Polish, Asian American and many other histories.
15. You know Texas pride and Texas self-criticism can coexist.

## Internal-link plan

Link individual observations to regional culture, barbecue, wildflowers, weather, high-school football, road trips and Texas history.

## Editorial note

Avoid demographic stereotypes, political litmus tests and claims that one accent, food preference or birthplace makes someone more Texan than another.

## Image guidance

Use one rights-cleared everyday Texas scene rather than a collage of clichés.
