# Practical Tips for Staying Safe Outdoors in a Texas Heat Wave

Texas heat can become dangerous long before an outing feels dramatic. The safest outdoor plan is built around **forecast conditions, exertion, shade, hydration, acclimatization and an exit plan**—not toughness.

This is a safety-sensitive article. Current National Weather Service, CDC and local emergency guidance must override any evergreen wording below.

## 1. Check the forecast and heat alerts before leaving

Look at the destination-specific forecast, not just the temperature shown on a phone home screen. Heat index, overnight lows, cloud cover, wind and local advisories all matter.

## 2. Move strenuous activity to the coolest part of the day

NWS heat-safety guidance recommends reducing or rescheduling strenuous activity during dangerous heat. Early morning is usually safer than mid-afternoon, but local conditions still control.

## 3. Do not use thirst as your only hydration trigger

Bring enough water for the actual route plus delay margin. People with medical conditions or fluid restrictions should follow clinician guidance rather than generic hydration advice.

## 4. Build shade into the route

A trail with trees, shelters or a short return path creates more options than an exposed ridgeline miles from the vehicle.

## 5. Wear clothing appropriate for heat and sun

Lightweight clothing, sun protection and a hat can reduce exposure. Technical choices should match the activity rather than fashion.

## 6. Acclimatization matters

Someone newly arrived from a cooler climate may not tolerate the same workload as a person accustomed to sustained Texas heat.

## 7. Slow down before symptoms force you to stop

A pace that feels easy at 70°F may be unsustainable at 100°F. Heat load compounds with sun, humidity, pack weight and terrain.

## 8. Know the difference between discomfort and emergency warning signs

Heat exhaustion and heat stroke are not interchangeable. Suspected heat stroke is a medical emergency. Current NWS/CDC emergency guidance should be displayed prominently when this page is promoted.

## 9. Never leave a child or pet in a parked vehicle

Vehicle temperatures can become dangerous rapidly. NWS repeatedly warns never to leave children unattended in vehicles, even briefly.

## 10. Treat dogs as heat-vulnerable partners

Hot pavement, limited shade and insufficient water can injure pets. Park rules and veterinarian guidance should control pet-specific decisions.

## 11. Carry more water in desert and exposed-country travel

West Texas routes can combine intense sun, low humidity, wind and long distances from services. A trailhead with no water is not unusual.

## 12. Tell someone where you are going

For remote hikes, paddles or drives, leave a route and expected return time with someone who is not on the trip.

## 13. Do not count on cell service

A safety plan that requires a working phone signal is not a complete plan in remote Texas.

## 14. Watch for heat plus other hazards

Heat can coincide with wildfire conditions, poor air quality, drought, venomous wildlife activity or sudden thunderstorms.

## 15. Turn around early

The most valuable heat-safety habit is willingness to abandon the plan. Reaching a summit, fishing spot or campsite is never worth pushing through escalating symptoms.

## A pre-trip heat checklist

Before leaving, answer:

- What is the forecast high and overnight low?
- Is there a heat advisory or warning?
- Where is reliable shade?
- How much water is available versus carried?
- What is the turnaround point?
- Who knows the route?
- What is the nearest reliable cooling location?
- Can the trip be moved to another day?
