# Texas Place Names and Words Locals Know How to Pronounce

Texas place names preserve layers of Spanish, Indigenous, German, Czech, French, Anglo-American and local history. That makes pronunciation less like a single statewide rulebook and more like a map of communities.

A useful guide should not mock visitors for getting names wrong. It should explain why local pronunciation sometimes differs from what spelling suggests and point readers toward the community itself when more than one pronunciation circulates.

## 1. Bexar

Bexar County is commonly pronounced roughly **“Bear.”** The spelling reflects the historic Spanish name San Fernando de Béxar.

## 2. Boerne

Boerne is generally pronounced **“BUR-nee.”** The Hill Country community’s German heritage helps explain why English phonics do not get a newcomer very far.

## 3. Gruene

Gruene, the historic district in New Braunfels, is commonly pronounced **“Green.”**

## 4. New Braunfels

Locals generally say **“New BRAWN-fels.”** The city grew from a German immigrant settlement, but place-name pronunciation evolved with the Texas community that uses it.

## 5. Refugio

Refugio is commonly pronounced **“Re-FURY-oh.”** The town and county name trace to Spanish-era history in the region.

## 6. Mexia

Mexia is famously pronounced **“Muh-HAY-uh.”** It is named for the Mexía family, which makes the local pronunciation more understandable than the English-looking spelling suggests.

## 7. Manor

The Central Texas city of Manor is generally pronounced **“MAY-ner.”**

## 8. Burnet

Burnet is commonly pronounced **“BURN-it.”** The old mnemonic “Burnet, durn it, learn it” survives because generations of Texans needed the reminder too.

## 9. Palestine

The East Texas city is commonly pronounced with a final syllable closer to **“-steen”** than the pronunciation many people use for the Middle Eastern geographic name.

## 10. Waxahachie

Waxahachie is commonly pronounced approximately **“WOK-suh-HATCH-ee.”** The deeper linguistic origin is debated in historical sources, so the article should not turn a colorful origin story into settled fact.

## 11. Nacogdoches

Nacogdoches is generally pronounced **“Nak-uh-DOH-chis.”** The name is tied to Caddo history, so the modern English pronunciation should not be confused with a definitive statement about the original Indigenous language.

## 12. Pflugerville

Pflugerville is commonly pronounced **“FLOO-ger-vill.”** The city name comes from the Pfluger family.

## 13. Llano

The Hill Country town and county are commonly pronounced **“LAN-oh.”** That differs from standard Spanish pronunciation of *llano*, another reminder that local Texas usage is its own evidence.

## 14. Guadalupe

Texas pronunciation varies by context and community, especially between English-dominant and Spanish-dominant speakers. A statewide guide should not declare one form universally correct.

## 15. Kuykendahl

The Houston-area road name Kuykendahl is commonly heard in forms close to **“KIR-ken-doll.”** Because family and neighborhood pronunciations can vary, this entry requires local verification immediately before publication.

## Why Texas pronunciation is complicated

Texas names arrived through different languages and eras. Some retained traces of an older language. Others were reshaped by English-speaking communities. Some evolved into pronunciations that make sense only after hearing locals use them for years.

That is normal place-name evolution, not evidence that one community “doesn’t know how to pronounce” its own name.

## A better rule for visitors

When in doubt:

1. listen to local residents;
2. check the city, county, university or local historical organization;
3. avoid correcting a community based only on dictionary rules from another language;
4. treat disputed pronunciations and origins as disputed rather than manufacturing certainty.
