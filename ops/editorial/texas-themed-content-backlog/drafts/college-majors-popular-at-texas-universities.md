# College Majors Texas Students Often Research — and How to Compare Them

A useful guide to college majors in Texas should do more than recycle a national “top 10 majors” list. Texas has a large, varied economy, hundreds of postsecondary institutions and major regional differences in labor demand.

The better question is not simply **which major is most popular?** It is: **which fields are producing large numbers of Texas graduates, which are tied to high-demand occupations, and which programs fit a student’s goals, cost tolerance and preferred part of the state?**

This draft is structured around fields families commonly encounter in Texas higher education. It should use the Texas Higher Education Coordinating Board (THECB) Degree Dashboard and current high-demand-field data for any numerical claims at publication time.

## 1. Business and management

Business remains one of the broadest degree families because it can lead toward accounting, finance, marketing, operations, supply-chain work, entrepreneurship and management.

In Texas, the field connects naturally to major corporate centers in Dallas–Fort Worth, Houston, Austin and San Antonio as well as small-business ownership statewide.

Students should compare:

- accounting/licensure pathways;
- finance and analytics concentrations;
- internship access;
- supply-chain or energy specialization;
- entrepreneurship programs;
- total program cost rather than school branding alone.

## 2. Nursing and health professions

Health-related programs matter across Texas because demand is not confined to the largest metros. Rural hospitals, community health systems, medical centers and rapidly growing suburbs all create different workforce needs.

The word “nursing” also hides several educational routes: vocational nursing, associate-degree RN programs, BSN programs and graduate specialties are not interchangeable.

Any publication version should link program claims to current Texas Board of Nursing and institution data rather than implying that every health degree leads to the same license.

## 3. Engineering

Texas gives engineering students unusually varied industry contexts: energy, aerospace, semiconductors, manufacturing, construction, transportation, water infrastructure and technology.

Useful comparisons include:

- mechanical;
- electrical;
- civil;
- chemical;
- petroleum;
- computer;
- aerospace;
- environmental engineering.

The best field depends on interests and the current labor market, not on a statewide ranking.

## 4. Computer science, information systems and cybersecurity

These programs can overlap, but they train for different roles.

Computer science generally emphasizes software, algorithms and computational theory. Information-systems programs often sit closer to business operations. Cybersecurity programs may emphasize networks, risk, defense and infrastructure.

Texas students can compare programs against the state’s technology, defense, finance, healthcare and government employers.

## 5. Education

Teacher preparation remains a major Texas workforce issue because the state operates one of the nation’s largest public-school systems.

A useful article should explain that “education major” can mean elementary preparation, secondary subject certification, special education, bilingual education, educational leadership or other pathways.

Teacher-certification requirements should always be checked against current TEA rules before publication.

## 6. Biology and life sciences

Biology attracts students headed toward research, healthcare, environmental work, biotechnology and professional-school prerequisites.

But a biology degree is not automatically a medical-school track. Families should compare the actual curriculum, undergraduate research access, advising, lab experience and post-graduation outcomes.

## 7. Agriculture and natural-resource fields

Texas makes this category broader than many students expect.

Possible programs include:

- agricultural economics;
- animal science;
- plant and soil science;
- range management;
- wildlife science;
- agricultural engineering;
- food science;
- agribusiness.

These majors connect naturally to TexasDefined coverage of ranching, water, wildlife, rural counties and the state economy.

## 8. Construction, architecture and the built environment

Fast-growing metros create demand for people who understand buildings and infrastructure, but the educational pathways differ.

Architecture, construction science, construction management, civil engineering, planning and real-estate programs prepare students for different roles. The article should explain those distinctions rather than grouping everything under “construction.”

## 9. Communications, media and design

Texas has major advertising, broadcasting, sports, entertainment, publishing and digital-media markets.

Students should compare portfolio expectations, internship pipelines and practical production opportunities. A degree title alone does not reveal whether a program is theory-heavy or production-heavy.

## 10. Criminal justice and public-service fields

Texas institutions offer criminal justice, emergency management, public administration, homeland security and related programs.

These fields should not be presented as automatic tickets to a government job. Hiring standards, civil-service requirements, background standards and agency qualifications vary.

## 11. Skilled technical and applied programs

A Texas higher-education guide should not treat bachelor’s degrees as the only valuable path.

THECB’s high-demand-field framework includes many credentials below the bachelor’s level. Community colleges and technical programs can prepare students for healthcare, advanced manufacturing, information technology, energy, transportation and other occupations.

That makes certificate and associate-degree comparisons an important part of the article.

## 12. How to compare a major before choosing it

For each field, students should check:

1. **Program cost** — tuition, fees, housing and time to completion.
2. **Accreditation or licensure relevance** — especially in nursing, engineering, education and other regulated fields.
3. **Graduation outcomes** — not just admission statistics.
4. **Internship geography** — where employers and clinical sites are actually located.
5. **Transferability** — particularly for community-college pathways.
6. **Debt** — compare borrowing with realistic early-career earnings.
7. **Current demand** — use state data rather than promotional copy.

## Texas-specific data layer to refresh before publication

Use THECB DataBridge as the source of record for statewide higher-education data. At promotion time, refresh:

- degree and certificate totals;
- degree-field distributions where available;
- credentials-of-value information;
- current high-demand fields;
- completion and debt context.

Do not label a field “the most popular in Texas” unless the current THECB data actually supports that exact statement.
