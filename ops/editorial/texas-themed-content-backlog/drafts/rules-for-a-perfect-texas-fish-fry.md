# Rules for a Texas Fish Fry That Is Crisp, Safe and Actually Fun to Host

A fish fry can be one of the easiest ways to feed a crowd, but hot oil, raw seafood and Texas heat make planning more important than bravado. The useful “rules” are about texture, timing and safety—not pretending one breading recipe is mandatory statewide.

## 1. Start with fish suited to frying

Catfish is closely associated with Southern and Texas fish fries, but other mild, firm fish can work. If using personally caught fish, follow current Texas fishing regulations and safe-cleaning guidance.

## 2. Keep raw fish cold

Texas A&M AgriLife recommends keeping raw fish and seafood at 40°F or colder during transport and outdoor preparation.

Use a separate sealed cooler or container so raw seafood cannot drip onto ready-to-eat foods.

## 3. Dry the fish before breading

Excess surface moisture makes coating less even and can worsen oil splatter.

Pat portions dry with disposable towels and keep raw-fish prep away from cooked-food service.

## 4. Pick a coating style and stay consistent

Cornmeal-forward breading is common for catfish. Flour blends, seasoned cornmeal and batter styles all exist.

The key is even coating and manageable portion size, not an “authenticity” argument.

## 5. Season the fish and the coating thoughtfully

Salt, black pepper, cayenne, paprika, garlic and other seasonings can build flavor. Avoid making spice so aggressive that the fish disappears.

## 6. Do not overcrowd the fryer

Adding too much cold food at once drops oil temperature and encourages greasy coating.

Cook in batches sized for the equipment.

## 7. Use equipment designed for the job

Set fryers on a stable, level, noncombustible surface away from children, pets and foot traffic. Follow the fryer manufacturer’s instructions for oil capacity, ignition and shutdown.

Never improvise a large outdoor oil-frying setup without equipment intended for that use.

## 8. Keep water away from hot oil

Water and hot oil are a dangerous combination. Dry utensils and food surfaces, keep drinks away from the fryer and do not attempt to extinguish a grease fire with water.

For current fire-safety guidance, verify recommendations from the fryer manufacturer, local fire authorities and NFPA/USFA before publication.

## 9. Use a food thermometer

A browned crust is not proof that fish is safely cooked. Use current USDA/AgriLife safe-temperature guidance for fish and seafood rather than relying only on color or flaking.

## 10. Create separate raw and cooked zones

Use different trays, tongs and work surfaces for raw fish and finished fish. AgriLife specifically warns against returning cooked food to a surface or marinade that contacted raw meat or seafood.

## 11. Serve in waves

Fried fish is best close to the fryer. Instead of piling a mountain of food under foil, serve smaller batches as they finish.

A wire rack can help preserve crispness better than sealing steam inside a container.

## 12. Build simple sides

A Texas fish-fry table can include:

- hush puppies;
- coleslaw;
- beans;
- potato salad kept cold;
- fries;
- pickles/onions;
- lemon;
- tartar sauce kept chilled;
- hot sauce.

## 13. Keep cold sides cold

Outdoor heat turns mayonnaise-based salads and sauces into a food-safety issue quickly. Use ice, insulated containers and current holding-time guidance.

## 14. Plan oil disposal before cooking

Never pour used cooking oil into a drain, storm sewer, yard or waterway. Let it cool completely and follow local waste/recycling guidance.

## 15. Make the fryer a controlled work zone

One experienced adult should own the fryer area. Guests can handle drinks, sides and serving; they do not need to crowd a vessel of hot oil.

That single rule improves both safety and timing.
