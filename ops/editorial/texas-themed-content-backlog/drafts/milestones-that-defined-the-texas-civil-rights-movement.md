# 15 Milestones That Defined the Texas Civil Rights Movement

Texas civil-rights history is not one march, one court case or one community. It includes Black Texans challenging white primaries and segregated universities, Mexican American Texans contesting school segregation and jury exclusion, students organizing sit-ins and walkouts, and lawyers turning local disputes into national constitutional cases.

This article should be published only after a source-by-source legal and historical review. The sequence below is a working editorial framework, not a substitute for primary records or case histories.

## 1. Reconstruction opened—and then narrowed—political participation

After the Civil War, Black Texans entered public life in ways that had been impossible under slavery. African American men voted and served in office during Reconstruction, but those gains were later attacked through violence, discriminatory election rules, segregation and disenfranchisement.

The civil-rights movement of the twentieth century makes more sense when readers understand that many activists were fighting to restore rights that had already been promised and then stripped away in practice.

## 2. Texas white-primary laws triggered a long legal fight

In 1923, Texas enacted a law excluding Black voters from Democratic Party primaries. Because the Democratic primary often determined the eventual officeholder in one-party Texas, exclusion from the primary was a powerful form of disfranchisement.

That law helped trigger a series of cases that would run for two decades.

## 3. Nixon v. Herndon struck the first major blow in 1927

El Paso physician Lawrence A. Nixon challenged Texas's white-primary law. In *Nixon v. Herndon*, the U.S. Supreme Court held the statute unconstitutional.

Texas then changed the legal mechanism of exclusion, which forced activists to keep litigating. That sequence is important: civil-rights victories often produced new barriers rather than immediate compliance.

## 4. Smith v. Allwright ended the white primary in 1944

Houston dentist Lonnie E. Smith challenged exclusion from the Democratic primary. The U.S. Supreme Court ruled in his favor in *Smith v. Allwright*.

Texas Historical Commission material describes the ruling as the decision that dismantled the white-primary system in Texas and influenced voting-rights struggles across the South.

## 5. Delgado v. Bastrop ISD challenged segregation of Mexican American students

In 1948, *Delgado v. Bastrop Independent School District* challenged the routine segregation of Mexican American children in Texas public schools.

The case is essential to a Texas civil-rights timeline because the state's history does not fit a simple Black-white framework. Mexican American families, lawyers and organizations built their own long record of school and voting-rights litigation.

## 6. Sweatt v. Painter forced integration of the University of Texas School of Law

Heman Marion Sweatt applied to the University of Texas School of Law and was denied admission because he was Black. Texas created a separate law school rather than admit him.

In 1950, the U.S. Supreme Court ruled that the separate legal education offered to Sweatt was not equal. The case became one of the major precedents leading toward *Brown v. Board of Education*.

## 7. Hernandez v. Texas broadened equal-protection doctrine in 1954

The U.S. Supreme Court's decision in *Hernandez v. Texas* arose from Jackson County and addressed systematic exclusion of Mexican Americans from juries.

The Court rejected the idea that Fourteenth Amendment equal protection was limited to discrimination against Black Americans. That made a Texas case nationally significant to the constitutional history of Mexican American civil rights.

## 8. Brown v. Board forced Texas districts to confront school segregation

The 1954 *Brown* decision was national, but implementation happened locally. Texas school districts responded unevenly, and desegregation was often delayed, contested or resisted.

TexasDefined should connect the national ruling to specific Texas schools and communities rather than implying that a Supreme Court decision instantly integrated the state.

## 9. The Mansfield school crisis exposed organized resistance

Mansfield became one of Texas's most visible school-desegregation confrontations in the 1950s. Resistance to a federal court order drew national attention and showed how local officials and communities could obstruct desegregation even after *Brown*.

This section requires careful sourcing and should avoid simplified claims about who acted when until verified against archival records.

## 10. Student sit-ins brought direct action to Texas in 1960

Black college students in Texas joined the sit-in movement challenging segregated lunch counters and public accommodations. Houston students associated with Texas Southern University and students in Marshall were among those who organized direct-action protests.

The Texas Historical Commission has documented Marshall as an important civil-rights center where students from historically Black colleges challenged segregation.

## 11. The Civil Rights Act of 1964 changed the legal landscape

The federal Civil Rights Act attacked discrimination in public accommodations, employment and other areas. President Lyndon B. Johnson, a Texan, signed the legislation after a long national struggle led by civil-rights activists.

Texas history should not reduce the law to the president who signed it; the movement that made the legislation politically possible belongs at the center of the story.

## 12. The Voting Rights Act of 1965 targeted barriers to the ballot

The federal Voting Rights Act created new enforcement tools against racial discrimination in voting. Its impact in Texas belongs in the same continuum as the earlier white-primary litigation.

For publication, distinguish the original 1965 law from later amendments and later court decisions affecting coverage and enforcement.

## 13. Barbara Jordan's 1966 election marked a political breakthrough

Barbara Jordan of Houston won election to the Texas Senate in 1966 and became the first African American elected to that chamber since Reconstruction. Texas State Library materials place her rise in the broader story of hard-won Black political participation.

Jordan later served in Congress and became one of the most nationally recognized Texas political figures of the twentieth century.

## 14. Mexican American student walkouts pushed school equality into a new phase

By the late 1960s, Mexican American students in South Texas and elsewhere organized walkouts over discriminatory discipline, unequal facilities, curriculum, language and representation.

These protests belong beside court cases because they show students themselves forcing institutions to respond rather than waiting for litigation alone.

## 15. The movement continued into voting, education and representation fights

There is no clean endpoint. Texas civil-rights history continued through battles over bilingual education, voting rights, redistricting, school finance, representation, employment and access to public institutions.

A good TexasDefined page should therefore treat the 1960s as a major phase, not the end of the story.

## A better way to teach this history

Organize the subject by mechanisms of change:

- **Litigation:** Nixon, Smith, Delgado, Sweatt, Hernandez and school-desegregation cases.
- **Direct action:** sit-ins, boycotts, walkouts and organizing.
- **Legislation:** federal civil- and voting-rights laws plus state/local responses.
- **Political representation:** the return and expansion of Black and Mexican American officeholding.
- **Local places:** schools, courthouses, rail depots, neighborhoods, colleges and churches where the movement happened.

That structure creates natural links to county and city pages and avoids presenting civil rights as a disconnected national chapter.
