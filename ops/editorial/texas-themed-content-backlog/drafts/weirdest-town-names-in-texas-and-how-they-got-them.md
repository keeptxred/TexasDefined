# 20 Weird Texas Town Names That Sound Made Up—but Aren’t

**Dek:** From Cut and Shoot and Dime Box to Bug Tussle, Nameless and Uncertain, Texas has place names that sound like punch lines. Their real stories involve post offices, cattle, dry creeks, railroads, family names—and a healthy amount of folklore.

Texas road maps have a sense of humor. You can leave Cut and Shoot, pass a sign for Dime Box, wonder whether you are really in Uncertain, and still have Bug Tussle waiting somewhere down the road. The names sound invented for a roadside comedy bit. Most were not.

Behind Texas’s strangest town names are ordinary forces that shaped communities across the state: post-office rules, railroad stops, ranch brands, family names, creeks, wildlife, arguments, jokes and stories repeated long enough to become local history. Sometimes the record is clear. Sometimes the best Texas historians can say is that several versions survived—and that is part of the fun.

One note before the road trip: “town” is used loosely here. Some of these places are incorporated cities, some are small unincorporated communities, and a few are former communities that survive mainly in maps and local memory. Every name on this list, however, belongs to a real Texas place.

## 1. Cut and Shoot — Montgomery County

This one really does trace back to a confrontation. The Handbook of Texas says the name apparently grew out of a 1912 community dispute that nearly turned violent. Accounts disagree about what started the argument—versions involve a church steeple, who should be allowed to preach and competing land claims—but one line survived the disagreement.

A young boy reportedly announced that he was going to “cut around the corner and shoot through the bushes.” The fight did not become the community’s legacy. The phrase did. Cut and Shoot eventually became the official name, and today it is an incorporated city north of Houston.

## 2. Dime Box — Lee County

Dime Box may have the most practical origin on the list. Before the community had a formal post office, residents reportedly left outgoing letters along with a dime in a box at a local office. The coin paid for weekly delivery to Giddings.

The routine became so familiar that “Dime Box” turned into a place name. Later, when the railroad established a new settlement nearby, the newer community took the Dime Box name and the original site became Old Dime Box. A tiny mail-payment system ended up naming two places.

## 3. Nameless — Travis County

Nameless sounds like somebody forgot to finish the map, but its name was a response to bureaucracy. According to the Handbook of Texas, residents repeatedly submitted proposed names for a post office and had them rejected. After six failures, they sent back an exasperated message telling postal officials, in effect, to let the post office remain nameless.

Washington took them literally. A post office called Nameless was established in 1880. Few Texas place names have a better paper trail—or a better example of frustration becoming geography.

## 4. Uncertain — Harrison County

Appropriately, nobody can say with certainty why Uncertain is called Uncertain. The Caddo Lake community has several competing origin stories, and the Handbook of Texas preserves more than one rather than pretending the mystery is settled.

One tradition says steamboat captains had trouble mooring at an “Uncertain Landing.” Another ties the word to confusion over citizenship before the boundary between the United States and the Republic of Texas was settled. A later explanation connects the name with uncertainty surrounding incorporation. The most accurate answer is also the most satisfying: the origin of Uncertain is uncertain.

## 5. Earth — Lamb County

Earth is real, and Texas history gives it several origin stories. The Handbook of Texas records traditions involving a sandstorm, a postal exchange in which “the earth seems to move,” and a proposed name of “Good Earth” inspired by the area’s soil.

The details do not resolve into one uncontested version, so the honest version is better than a neat one: several stories explain how this Lamb County community ended up with the biggest possible name.

## 6. Happy — Swisher County

Happy is less whimsical once you know the landscape. The town took its name from Happy Draw. According to the Handbook of Texas, cowboys were so pleased to find water there that they called the drainage Happy Draw.

On the High Plains, where a dependable water source could determine a route, a camp or a settlement, “Happy” was not marketing. It was relief.

## 7. Ding Dong — Bell County

Ding Dong is a real Bell County community. The Handbook of Texas confirms the place, while the colorful naming story comes from local-history accounts rather than the Handbook’s main entry.

The tradition says two Bell cousins operated a country store and had a sign painted with two bells bearing their initials, with “Ding Dong” worked into the design. Customers began using the store’s nickname for the surrounding community. It is a good story—and because the detailed version rests on local tradition, it belongs in the “widely told” category rather than the “case closed” category.

## 8. Bug Tussle — Fannin County

Bug Tussle is another name with competing folklore. The community was first known as Truss, but the Handbook of Texas records several stories for the later name. One involves insects ruining an ice-cream social. Another says picnickers watched bugs fighting. A third has residents settling a naming argument after seeing two tumblebugs tussle.

There is no need to pick a winner. The fact that multiple bug-fight stories survived is more revealing than any single punch line: small communities often carried their histories through oral tradition long after the original moment was gone.

## 9. Notrees — Ector County

Notrees sounds like a West Texas joke, and the landscape certainly helps the name land. But the Handbook of Texas gives it a specific local story. The site once had a lone native tree, which was later removed when a Shell gas plant was built.

When grocer and first postmaster Charlie Brown supplied a name for the post office in 1946, “Notrees” fit. The name turned a vanished landmark into a permanent one.

## 10. Point Blank — San Jacinto County

Point Blank sounds like it should come with a Western shootout, but its history points in a different direction. The Handbook of Texas traces the name to Florence Dissiway, a Frenchwoman who called the place “Blanc Point.” Local usage reversed the order into Point Blank.

That linguistic flip produced one of the state’s most cinematic-sounding community names without any gunfight required.

## 11. Whiteface — Cochran County

Whiteface is ranching history hiding in plain sight. The town developed around the Whiteface Camp and Whiteface Pasture associated with cattleman C. C. Slaughter. The Handbook of Texas ties the name to the white-faced cattle on the ranch.

What sounds unusual to a modern traveler would have made immediate sense in a landscape organized around ranches, cattle and brands.

## 12. Looneyville — Nacogdoches County

No, Looneyville was not named because its residents were considered “loony.” The East Texas community was named for John Looney, an early settler and storekeeper.

That makes Looneyville a useful reminder that some of Texas’s funniest-looking names are simply surnames that changed meaning to later ears.

## 13. Noodle — Jones County

Noodle took its name from nearby Noodle Creek. The Handbook of Texas also preserves the local tradition that “noodle” was used to mean “nothing,” a reference to a creek bed that could be dry.

Whether or not a traveler expects pasta, the name is rooted in the water—or lack of it—that people used to describe the country.

## 14. Muleshoe — Bailey County

Muleshoe was named for the nearby Muleshoe Ranch when the railroad reached the area in 1913. The town story is straightforward; the ranch name is less so.

The Handbook of Texas says the precise origin of the ranch name is obscure, though traditions connect it to a found mule shoe and to the ranch’s distinctive branding history. The important part is that a ranch name became a railroad-town name and then the identity of a county seat.

## 15. Tarzan — Martin County

Tarzan did not begin as a movie-set nickname. In 1927, local resident Tant Lindsay submitted possible names for a post office, and postal officials selected Tarzan. The Handbook of Texas documents that basic sequence.

Local retellings connect the suggestion to the popularity of Edgar Rice Burroughs’s Tarzan stories, but the safest historical point is the one the record supports: Tarzan was deliberately submitted as a community name and accepted by the Post Office.

## 16. Turkey — Hall County

Turkey began as Turkey Roost, a name tied to wild turkeys that roosted along nearby Turkey Creek. When a post office was established in 1893, the name was shortened to Turkey.

The Panhandle town later became closely associated with Western swing musician Bob Wills, but its unusual name reaches back to wildlife and the creek country that preceded the town.

## 17. Nada — Colorado County

Nada looks like the Spanish word for “nothing,” but that is not the origin recorded by the Handbook of Texas. The settlement was first known as Vox Populi, and TSHA says the later name Nada was an Americanized form of a Czech word rendered in the historical account as “najda,” meaning hope.

The result is a name that modern travelers may read one way while the community’s immigrant history points somewhere else entirely.

## 18. Fluvanna — Scurry County

Fluvanna sounds invented, but its origin is more conventional than its sound. The Scurry County community was named for Fluvanna County, Virginia, the home county of a surveyor involved in the area.

Texas is full of place names carried west by settlers, railroad employees, surveyors and developers. Fluvanna is one of the stranger-sounding examples of that familiar American habit.

## 19. Frognot — Collin County

Frognot may have the richest official collection of competing explanations on this list. A Texas Historical Commission marker says the rural community has also been known as Frog Not, Frog Nod and Frogknot—and records several theories tied to frogs in nearby Indian Creek.

One story says a strict schoolmaster would not let children bring frogs to school. Another says residents nearly eradicated an overabundant frog population. A third says loud frogs kept people awake as they “nodded” off. Yet another explanation involves a railroad switch, also called a frog, that residents did not want. The state marker does not crown a winner, and neither should we.

## 20. Hoop and Holler — Liberty County

Hoop and Holler deserves a place here because the name itself is not an internet invention. Texas place-name records and the Texas Almanac recognize it as a former Liberty County community, even though it no longer exists as an active town.

What is harder to find is a reliable, authoritative account explaining exactly how the name began. Rather than borrow an unsourced tale because it sounds good, this is one mystery worth leaving open. Texas has enough documented weirdness without manufacturing any.

## Why does Texas have so many strange place names?

The pattern is less random than it first appears. In the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, a community often needed a unique postal name. Railroads created new townsites and moved commercial centers. Ranches supplied names to settlements. Families lent surnames that sound different a century later. Creeks, animals and scarce water left their mark. And when nobody wrote down the definitive story, oral tradition filled the gap.

- **Post-office bureaucracy** produced names such as Nameless and helped formalize many others.
- **Railroads** relocated or created communities, as happened around Dime Box and Muleshoe.
- **Ranching and livestock** shaped names such as Whiteface and Muleshoe.
- **Water and landscape** appear in stories behind Happy, Noodle and Notrees.
- **Family names** can sound funny later, as Looneyville demonstrates.
- **Folklore** survives most strongly where the written record is thin, which is why Bug Tussle, Ding Dong, Frognot and Uncertain deserve careful wording.

## The best stories are the ones we do not have to exaggerate

It is tempting to treat a list like this as pure novelty, but these names are miniature history lessons. A dime left with a letter tells you how rural mail worked. A place named Happy tells you what finding water meant on the Plains. A ranch name becoming a county seat explains how cattle and railroads organized West Texas. An argument over a post-office name can still be visible on a highway sign 140 years later.

And sometimes the uncertainty is the history. If three generations told three versions of why Bug Tussle got its name, the responsible answer is not to choose the funniest one. It is to preserve the fact that the stories competed.

### Hoop and Holler sourcing caution

The existence of Hoop and Holler is documented, but no authoritative naming-origin account was located during this research pass. Do not add a colorful origin claim unless a stronger primary or local-historical source is found.
