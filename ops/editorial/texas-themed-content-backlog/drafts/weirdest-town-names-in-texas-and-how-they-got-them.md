# Texas Town Names That Deserve Their Own Explanation

Texas has enough unusual place names to fill a road atlas, but the stories behind them are often repeated more confidently than the historical record allows. This article should separate documented naming history from local tradition and folklore.

## Cut and Shoot — Montgomery County

The Handbook of Texas traces the name to a 1912 community confrontation that nearly became violent. Different versions disagree about the dispute itself, but a boy reportedly threatened to “cut around the corner and shoot through the bushes.” The phrase stuck.

The important editorial detail is that even TSHA preserves multiple versions of the underlying dispute.

## Dime Box — Lee County

Before a formal post office existed, residents reportedly placed outgoing mail and a dime in a small box for weekly delivery to Giddings. When postal confusion with Brown’s Mill/Brownsville became a problem, the community adopted Dime Box.

The later railroad settlement became Dime Box while the original site became Old Dime Box.

## Nameless — Travis County

TSHA reports that residents submitted multiple names for a post office and were repeatedly rejected. After six failures they wrote back telling postal officials, in effect, to let the place remain nameless. A post office named Nameless was established in 1880.

This is one of the rare great Texas place-name stories for which the institutional history is unusually specific.

## Uncertain — Harrison County

Uncertain, on Caddo Lake, has several competing origin stories. One tradition connects the name to steamboat captains having trouble mooring at “Uncertain Landing.” Another ties it to uncertainty over citizenship before the U.S.–Republic of Texas boundary was settled. Former officials offered yet another explanation tied to incorporation.

The correct editorial treatment is not to pick the funniest version—it is to say the origin is genuinely uncertain.

## Earth — Lamb County

TSHA records several stories for Earth. One links the name to a sandstorm, another to a postal reply saying “the earth seems to move,” and another to the region’s fertile soil and a proposed “Good Earth” name.

This is another entry where multiple traditions should remain multiple traditions.

## Happy — Swisher/Randall County area

Happy takes its name from Happy Draw. TSHA says cowboys were happy to find water there, giving the draw—and eventually the town—its name.

In a dry Plains landscape, the name is less whimsical once the water story is understood.

## Ding Dong — Bell County

Ding Dong is a real Bell County community. The Handbook of Texas confirms the place but gives only limited naming detail on its main entry. Any colorful origin story should be sourced separately before publication rather than copied from unsourced travel trivia.

## Turkey — Hall County

Turkey is another real Panhandle community whose name is tied in local history to wild turkeys in the area. Verify the exact naming account with TSHA/local historical sources before promotion.

## Why these names matter

Odd names are more than novelty. They often preserve traces of:

- post-office bureaucracy;
- railroad relocation;
- water sources;
- local arguments;
- wildlife;
- family names;
- jokes that became official;
- conflicting oral traditions.

That makes a town-name article a useful bridge into county pages and local history instead of a throwaway social list.
