# 12 Texas Hill Country Summer Camps for Families to Research

Texas Hill Country summer camps are part of a century-old regional tradition, but a 2026 guide cannot be written like an old nostalgia roundup. Camp licensing, emergency planning, flood risk, communications, evacuation procedures, medical support, waterfront rules and the exact physical location of sleeping cabins matter as much as horseback riding, archery or camp songs.

This is therefore a **research shortlist, not a ranking or safety endorsement**. Before a camp appears in a published TexasDefined recommendation, verify its current status in the Texas Department of State Health Services Youth Camp Program and review the camp's current safety information directly.

## The 2026 rule: verify the license first

Texas DSHS is the principal state authority for health and safety conditions at qualifying licensed youth camps. DSHS says qualifying youth camps must obtain a license before operating. For the 2026 season, new and existing youth camps were required to complete a pre-licensing inspection, and camps can also receive random unannounced inspections during the season.

DSHS maintains an Active Youth Camps roster and a public license-search tool. The roster was updated August 14, 2026 and can also show an application as pending.

A camp's own website saying it is licensed is useful, but families should still verify the current DSHS record.

## 1. Camp Longhorn — Burnet area

Camp Longhorn operates multiple Hill Country sites, including Inks Lake-area programs. Its official site currently publishes 2026 term dates for Inks Lake, Indian Springs and C3.

Why research it: long-running Texas camp tradition, multiweek residential structure and a wide activity mix.

Before enrollment: verify the exact site—not merely the Camp Longhorn brand—because emergency planning, waterfront setting, cabin location and transportation can differ by campus.

## 2. Camp Waldemar — Hunt

Camp Waldemar is a residential girls camp on the Guadalupe River near Hunt. Its official site publishes 2026 dates and states that it is licensed by DSHS and belongs to the Camp Association for Mutual Progress.

Why research it: historic Hill Country camp tradition, multiweek and shorter introductory terms, horseback riding, land/water sports and arts programming.

Before enrollment: use the DSHS lookup to verify the current license and review the camp's current safety-reporting/emergency information.

## 3. Camp Stewart for Boys — Hunt

Camp Stewart is one of the long-established camps in the Hunt area and is commonly associated with the traditional Hill Country residential-camp model.

Why research it: boys' residential program, horsemanship/outdoor traditions and a location deeply tied to the Guadalupe River camp corridor.

Before enrollment: verify 2026/next-season DSHS status, exact sleeping-cabin location, flood/emergency plan, alert system and current session details.

## 4. Camp La Junta — Hunt

Camp La Junta is another Hunt-area residential camp with a long Texas history.

Why research it: traditional boys' camp format and Hill Country outdoor programming.

Before enrollment: verify DSHS licensing, current operating dates, emergency plan, waterfront procedures and staff qualifications rather than relying on an old alumni or travel article.

## 5. Camp Balcones Springs — Marble Falls area

Camp Balcones Springs operates in the Highland Lakes/Hill Country region.

Why research it: broad activity mix and a location accessible from Austin and Central Texas.

Before enrollment: confirm current DSHS status and the emergency/communications procedures for the exact 2026 or future session under consideration.

## 6. Camp Champions — Marble Falls / Lake LBJ area

Camp Champions is a residential camp in the Marble Falls/Highland Lakes area.

Why research it: lake-oriented programming, established residential sessions and proximity to Central Texas metros.

Before enrollment: verify waterfront qualifications, lightning/weather protocols, evacuation routes, DSHS license and current medical staffing information.

## 7. Camp OTX — Bandera area

Camp OTX operates in the Hill Country near Bandera.

Why research it: modern residential-camp facilities and a large outdoor/recreation program.

Before enrollment: treat polished facilities as only one part of due diligence. Verify licensing, emergency plans, communications redundancy, water safety and transportation procedures.

## 8. Camp Peniel — Marble Falls area

Camp Peniel is a faith-based residential camp in the Highland Lakes/Hill Country region.

Why research it: families seeking explicitly Christian camp programming may want a different culture and daily schedule from a secular camp.

Before enrollment: compare faith expectations alongside the same DSHS, emergency, staffing and waterfront questions used for every other camp.

## 9. Lonehollow — Hunt area

Lonehollow is a Hill Country residential camp near Hunt.

Why research it: wide activity selection and a traditional sleepaway format.

Before enrollment: verify current license status, exact site conditions, emergency communications, floodplain considerations and medical policies.

## 10. Mo-Ranch — Hunt

Mo-Ranch is a Presbyterian conference center and camp property on the Guadalupe River that hosts youth programming.

Why research it: faith-based programming, river setting and a larger retreat/conference property.

Important: not every youth program marketed as a “camp” necessarily falls under the same DSHS youth-camp license definition. Verify the specific program and regulatory status, not just the property name.

## 11. Rocky River Ranch — Wimberley

Rocky River Ranch is a girls' camp in the Wimberley area.

Why research it: a smaller Hill Country setting and traditional residential-camp activities.

Before enrollment: verify current licensing, session dates, emergency planning and water/weather procedures directly.

## 12. Texas Lions Camp — Kerrville

Texas Lions Camp serves children with physical disabilities and certain medical conditions, including diabetes, through specialized camp programming.

Why research it: mission-driven programming designed around campers who may need more specialized support than a conventional residential camp provides.

Before enrollment: families should evaluate the exact program's eligibility, medical support, staffing and current regulatory status rather than comparing it to general-population camps on price or activity count alone.

## What changed after the 2025 Hill Country floods

Texas enacted new youth-camp safety legislation in September 2025: the Youth CAMPER Act (HB 1) and Heaven's 27 Camp Safety Act (SB 1). DSHS adopted implementing youth-camp rules effective in 2026.

The current rules and statutes strengthened requirements around licensing, emergency preparedness, communications and flood-related risks. DSHS also required pre-licensing inspections for existing as well as new qualifying camps for the 2026 season.

The regulatory details are too important to paraphrase casually. Before this page is published for any future camp season, re-read the current DSHS law/rule pages and update the parent checklist accordingly.

## Questions parents should ask every camp in 2026 and beyond

### Licensing and inspection

- Is this exact camp/site listed as currently licensed by DSHS, if the program meets the statutory youth-camp definition?
- When was its latest inspection?
- Is the license active, pending, expired or associated with a different physical site?

### Flood and severe-weather planning

- Are any camper sleeping cabins in a mapped floodplain or near a floodway?
- What is the camp's current written emergency plan?
- Where do campers evacuate during flash-flood, tornado or severe-thunderstorm threats?
- Are nighttime evacuation routes illuminated and usable during a power outage?
- How does the camp receive National Weather Service/local emergency alerts?

### Communication

- How will parents be notified during an emergency?
- What redundant phone/internet/radio systems exist if one network fails?
- Who has authority to order evacuation rather than shelter-in-place?

### Staff and medical care

- What counselor age/training standards apply?
- What medical professionals are on site or on call?
- How far is the nearest emergency department?
- What are the camp's medication, allergy and chronic-condition procedures?

### Waterfront and specialized activities

- What qualifications are required for lifeguards, boating staff, horseback instructors, climbing/rappelling staff and firearm instructors?
- What stops an activity when weather or water conditions deteriorate?

### Transportation and reunification

- How are campers transported if the property must be evacuated?
- Where is the off-site reunification location?
- How does the camp account for every camper during movement?

## Accreditation and association membership are useful—but not a license

Private camp associations and accreditation programs can add standards or peer networks. They do not replace Texas licensing when a program is legally required to hold a DSHS youth-camp license.

TexasDefined should therefore display association membership, accreditation and DSHS licensing as separate facts.
