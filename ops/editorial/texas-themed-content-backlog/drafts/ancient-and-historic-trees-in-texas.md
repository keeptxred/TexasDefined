# 10 Ancient and Historic Trees in Texas Worth Knowing

A historic tree is not automatically the oldest or largest tree in the state. Its importance can come from age, size, a documented event, a community story or a connection to a place that changed around it.

Texas A&M Forest Service maintains a Famous Trees of Texas program precisely because trees can serve as living historical markers. Some survive; others are remembered through documentation or descendant trees. This article should make that distinction explicit.

## 1. The Big Tree — Goose Island State Park

The giant coastal live oak at Goose Island State Park near Rockport is one of Texas’s most famous living trees. Texas A&M Forest Service notes that it was once recognized as the largest live oak in the United States and has been estimated at roughly a millennium old.

Because age estimates and national size rankings can change, publish them as sourced estimates rather than immutable records.

## 2. Treaty Oak — Austin

Treaty Oak is the surviving member of a grove remembered as the Council Oaks. The tree has been tied to Indigenous gatherings, local treaty stories and later Austin history.

Texas A&M Forest Service documents its preservation as a city park and its status as a major historic tree. Any specific treaty claim should be sourced carefully because oral tradition and later commemoration can become blurred.

## 3. Founders’ Oak — New Braunfels

Founders’ Oak near Comal Springs is tied to the founding era of New Braunfels and German immigration to Central Texas.

Texas A&M Forest Service connects the tree with Prince Carl of Solms-Braunfels and the land associated with the new settlement in 1845.

## 4. Kissing Oak — San Marcos

The Kissing Oak became associated with Sam Houston’s 1857 gubernatorial campaign visit to San Marcos, when local women presented him with a Texas flag and he thanked them with kisses after his speech.

The tree offers a human-scale way to connect political history to a living landscape.

## 5. Ranger Oaks — Seguin

Large live oaks in Seguin known as the Ranger Oaks are associated with early frontier/ranger activity dating to the Mexican Texas and Republic-era period.

A final article should contextualize ranger history carefully rather than using old commemorative language without discussing frontier conflict.

## 6. Sam Houston Oak — Gonzales-area retreat route

Texas A&M Forest Service documents a Sam Houston Oak tied to the Texian army’s retreat after the fall of the Alamo. Trees associated with the Runaway Scrape and Houston’s movements can help readers understand the Revolution as geography rather than a sequence of isolated battles.

Exact access should be verified before directing visitors to rural locations.

## 7. Houston Campsite Oak — Grapevine Springs

The Houston Campsite Oak is associated with Sam Houston’s 1843 effort to meet Native leaders and commissioners at Grapevine Springs for peace negotiations.

The tree provides a valuable entry into Republic-era diplomacy, which is often overshadowed by military history.

## 8. Which Way Tree — Harris County region

The Which Way Tree is connected by tradition to Sam Houston’s decision-making during the 1836 campaign before San Jacinto.

Because stories around famous trees can become embellished over generations, use Texas A&M Forest Service documentation and clearly label legend where evidence is uncertain.

## 9. Urrea Oaks — Refugio County

The Urrea Oaks are tied to the 1836 campaign in South Texas and events leading toward the Goliad tragedy.

This is another example of why tree history can be more than quirky trivia: living trees can anchor battlefield and campaign geography that has otherwise changed dramatically.

## 10. Stephen F. Austin-associated live oaks — Brazoria County

Texas A&M Forest Service documents live oaks connected to the site where Stephen F. Austin spent his final days near West Columbia.

The strongest version of this entry should verify exactly which trees survive, their documented provenance and current public-access status before publication.

## Historic trees need careful sourcing

Tree stories are particularly vulnerable to folklore. A tree may be genuinely old but not as old as a sign claims. A famous person may have visited the site without the exact conversation later attributed to them.

For each published profile, separate:

- measured tree size;
- estimated age;
- documented historical event;
- oral tradition or legend;
- current survival status;
- public versus private access.

## Build the tree data into county pages

The Famous Trees of Texas program can enrich county pages with specific local stories. A structured field could eventually record:

- tree name;
- species;
- county/city;
- historical significance;
- living/not living/descendant status;
- public access;
- authoritative source;
- coordinates only when appropriate and public.

That turns this list into reusable local data.
