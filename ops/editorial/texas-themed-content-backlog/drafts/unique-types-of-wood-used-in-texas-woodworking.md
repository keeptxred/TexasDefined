# Texas Woods Used in Furniture, Turning and Craft Work

Texas woodworkers can draw from native hardwoods, regional softwoods, reclaimed lumber and imported stock. “Texas wood” should mean the species or material has a documented Texas connection—not that every board sold in Texas was grown here.

## 1. Mesquite

Mesquite is one of the most recognizable Texas woodworking species. It can produce striking color and figure, but irregular grain, checking and small trunk dimensions affect how it is milled and used.

## 2. Pecan

Pecan is the state tree and a useful hardwood for furniture, cabinetry, turning and smaller craft work.

## 3. Live oak

Live oak is culturally iconic and extremely dense. Urban-tree salvage may create woodworking material, but tree-removal laws, disease concerns and provenance should be respected.

## 4. Post oak

Post oak has deep ties to Texas prairie and Cross Timbers landscapes and can be used in furniture and rustic work when properly dried.

## 5. White oak

White-oak species occur in parts of Texas and are also widely available commercially. Distinguish locally harvested stock from generic lumber-yard material.

## 6. Red oak

Several red-oak species occur in Texas. Grain, hardness and movement vary by species, so “Texas red oak” is too broad for technical woodworking claims.

## 7. Black walnut

Black walnut occurs in Texas, though commercial walnut stock may come from elsewhere. Its dark color makes it popular for furniture and accent work.

## 8. Cedar elm

Cedar elm is widespread and sometimes milled from urban or storm-damaged trees. Interlocked grain can make it challenging but visually interesting.

## 9. Sugarberry / hackberry

Common Texas trees can become useful lumber when carefully dried, though they are less famous than walnut or mesquite.

## 10. Eastern redcedar

Despite the name, eastern redcedar is a juniper. Aromatic heartwood is commonly used for chests, lining and small projects.

## 11. Ashe juniper

Central Texas “cedar” is usually Ashe juniper. Its ecological role is complex, and woodworking use should not be framed as a simplistic conservation solution.

## 12. Bald cypress

Native to wet East Texas and river/coastal habitats, cypress has a long history in regional construction and outdoor applications.

## 13. Southern yellow pine

East Texas pine forests underpin a major timber industry. Pine is central to framing, millwork and utilitarian woodworking even if it lacks the boutique image of mesquite.

## 14. Bois d’arc / Osage orange

Dense, durable and vivid when freshly cut, bois d’arc has historic use in fence posts, bows and specialty woodworking.

## 15. Reclaimed Texas lumber

Old barns, industrial buildings, rail structures and demolished houses may yield historic lumber, but “reclaimed” does not automatically mean safe or legal to salvage. Lead paint, chemicals, nails and ownership/provenance matter.

## Choosing a species

Woodworkers should consider:

- moisture content;
- stability;
- grain direction;
- hardness;
- tool wear;
- allergies/dust;
- finishing behavior;
- legal/provenance issues;
- whether outdoor durability is actually required.

## Source / verification notes

- Texas A&M Forest Service tree/species resources: https://tfsweb.tamu.edu/
- USDA Plants / Forest Service sources can support range and wood-property claims.
- Do not claim lumber is Texas-grown without provenance.
- Tree removal, transport and disease restrictions can be local or species-specific; reverify where relevant.
- Technical structural uses require grading/code sources, not this craft guide.

## Image guidance

Use one rights-cleared close photograph of a documented Texas-grown wood species or finished Texas-made piece with visible grain. Avoid an unlabeled lumber collage.
