# Places and Producers to Know When Buying Texas Pecans

Pecans are native to Texas and deeply tied to river bottoms, family orchards, commercial agriculture and the state’s food identity. The useful shopping question is not “Who is objectively best?” but **how to verify that a seller is actually connected to Texas pecan production and what form of pecan you want to buy**.

## Start with the Texas Pecan Growers Association

The Texas Pecan Growers Association maintains a current **Where to Buy** tool and producer/supplier listings. That should be the first verification layer before this article names any operating orchard, store or mail-order business.

## Producer examples worth researching

The final promoted article can include a rotating set of currently verified TPGA-linked growers and suppliers. Examples appearing in TPGA member/producer material include operations such as:

- Rio Bravo Pecan Co.;

These names are **research leads**, not permanent endorsements. Current retail availability, orchard location and shipping status must be checked before publication.

## 1. Buy in-shell when freshness and storage matter

In-shell pecans can store well when kept cool and dry. Buyers should inspect for mold, rancid odor or insect damage.

## 2. Shelled halves are convenient but need good storage

Once shelled, pecans are more exposed to oxygen and temperature. Current USDA/AgriLife storage guidance should support any shelf-life claims.

## 3. Pieces are useful for baking

Pecan pieces can be a better value for pies, cookies, pralines and savory recipes where perfect halves are unnecessary.

## 4. Native versus improved varieties

Texas growers may sell native seedlings or named improved cultivars. Flavor, nut size, shell thickness and harvest characteristics differ.

## 5. Harvest timing matters

Texas pecan harvest generally occurs in fall, but exact timing varies by region and cultivar. Fresh-crop marketing should be date-labeled.

## 6. San Saba has a strong pecan identity

San Saba’s connection to pecan growing makes it a natural road-trip anchor, but the article should link the town’s history to current growers rather than relying only on branding slogans.

## 7. Comanche and Central Texas also have deep orchard traditions

Pecan production is distributed across multiple Texas regions, especially where suitable bottomland, irrigation and orchard conditions exist.

## 8. Far West Texas can produce pecans too

Irrigated orchards in the Pecos/El Paso/Far West Texas region demonstrate how pecan agriculture extends beyond the stereotypical Central Texas river-bottom image.

## 9. Farmers markets can be useful—but verify origin

A Texas farmers market does not guarantee every pecan sold there was grown in Texas. Ask the vendor where the nuts were grown and processed.

## 10. Gift shops may sell Texas-branded pecans from third parties

Packaging that says “Texas” can refer to company identity rather than farm origin. The final article should teach readers to distinguish the two.

## 11. Flavored pecans are a different product category

Candied, roasted, spicy and chocolate-coated pecans can be excellent gifts, but they are not a substitute for comparing raw pecan quality.

## 12. Shipping conditions matter

Warm-weather shipping can affect nuts and coatings. Current producer shipping practices should be checked rather than generalized.

## Questions to ask a pecan seller

- Were these pecans grown in Texas?
- What harvest year/crop are they from?
- Are they native or a named cultivar?
- Are they shelled locally?
- How should they be stored?
- Are flavored products made in-house or private-labeled?
