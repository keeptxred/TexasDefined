# 15 Things You May Not Know About the Republic of Texas Era

The Republic of Texas is often compressed into a simple story: Texas won independence in 1836, existed as its own country for nine years, and joined the United States in 1845.

All three statements are broadly true. They also skip almost everything that made the Republic difficult, unusual and historically important: foreign diplomacy, debt, unsettled borders, repeated annexation attempts, conflict with Mexico, relations with Native nations, shifting capitals and the practical problems of building a government while the new nation was still fighting to survive.

Here are fifteen facts that add useful context to the Republic era without turning myth into history.

## 1. Texas declared independence before the Battle of San Jacinto

Delegates met at Washington-on-the-Brazos on March 1, 1836, while the Alamo was under siege. They adopted the Texas Declaration of Independence on March 2, formed an ad interim government and drafted a constitution.

The decisive victory at San Jacinto did not occur until April 21. In other words, the Republic was declared before the revolution's military outcome was secure.

## 2. San Jacinto created de facto independence, but Mexico did not simply accept the new republic

The Texian victory at San Jacinto and capture of Antonio López de Santa Anna broke the immediate Mexican military campaign and gave Texas de facto independence.

But Mexican governments did not recognize Texas independence as a settled permanent fact. The Republic lived with continuing military and diplomatic uncertainty throughout much of its existence.

## 3. Texans wanted U.S. annexation almost immediately

The later annexation debate can make it sound as if Texas spent nine years happily committed to permanent nationhood and only changed direction at the end.

The Texas State Library's annexation timeline shows otherwise. In September 1836, voters overwhelmingly favored annexation to the United States. A formal annexation offer followed in 1837.

## 4. The United States initially hesitated to annex Texas

Annexation was politically explosive in the United States, especially because of slavery, relations with Mexico and sectional politics.

The U.S. recognized the Republic of Texas in March 1837 but did not immediately absorb it. Texas withdrew its first formal annexation offer in 1838 after U.S. inaction.

## 5. The Republic conducted real international diplomacy

Texas was not merely waiting around to become a U.S. state. It sent diplomatic representatives abroad and maintained legations connected to Washington, London and Paris.

Texas State Library collections preserve the Republic's legation papers, showing officials negotiating recognition, trade, boundaries, annexation and relations with Mexico.

## 6. France, Britain and other powers mattered to Texas strategy

Recognition and commercial relationships with European powers gave the Republic international legitimacy and potential leverage in annexation negotiations.

Britain was especially relevant because British policymakers had economic, diplomatic and anti-slavery interests in the region. The Republic's foreign policy therefore connected Texas to much larger Atlantic-world politics.

## 7. The capital moved several times

Texas government did not begin with one settled capital city. During the revolutionary and Republic years, government functions moved through multiple locations before Austin emerged as the capital.

This instability was not mere indecision. War, security, geography and political rivalry all mattered.

## 8. The Archives War was a real political conflict over the capital

In 1842, President Sam Houston sought to move government archives away from Austin because of security concerns during renewed Mexican incursions.

Austin residents resisted. Angelina Eberly famously fired a cannon to alert the city when officials attempted to remove the archives. The confrontation helped preserve Austin's claim as the seat of government and became one of the Republic era's most memorable civic episodes.

## 9. The Republic carried heavy public debt

Independence did not produce financial stability. The young nation needed money for defense, government and diplomacy, while revenue remained limited.

Texas State Library material on the annexation settlement notes that the Republic carried nearly $10 million in public debt by the time of annexation. The debt problem remained intertwined with the disposition of Texas public lands after statehood.

## 10. Texas kept control of its public lands when it entered the Union

Texas's annexation arrangement differed from the admission of many other states. The state retained its public lands rather than transferring them wholesale to the federal government.

Those lands later became central to debt settlement, railroads, schools, universities and the Permanent School Fund. The Republic era therefore shaped financial institutions that still matter in Texas.

## 11. Annexation by treaty failed in the U.S. Senate

The United States and Texas negotiated an annexation treaty in 1844, but the U.S. Senate rejected it 35–16.

Annexation ultimately proceeded through a joint resolution of Congress rather than a ratified bilateral annexation treaty. That procedural history is one reason simplistic statements about "the treaty that annexed Texas" can be misleading.

## 12. Texas had an alternative independence offer from Mexico in 1845

As U.S. annexation moved forward, Mexico offered terms that would recognize Texas independence if Texas remained a separate republic.

The Texas State Library's timeline records the Cuevas-Smith agreement in May 1845. Texas leaders ultimately chose the U.S. annexation path instead.

## 13. Annexation was approved through several steps, not one dramatic signature

The U.S. Congress passed an annexation resolution in early 1845. Texas President Anson Jones received the offer. The Texas Congress accepted it. A convention approved it in July, and Texas voters approved annexation and a state constitution in October.

President James K. Polk signed the congressional resolution admitting Texas on December 29, 1845.

## 14. The Republic's government did not disappear on December 29, 1845

December 29 is the formal date Texas became the 28th state, but the governmental transition continued into 1846.

The Texas State Library timeline gives February 19, 1846, as the completion of the transfer of government, when Governor J. Pinckney Henderson took the oath of office and President Anson Jones ended the Republic government.

## 15. The Republic era cannot be understood only as Anglo-Texan political history

Tejanos, Native nations, enslaved people, free Black residents, immigrants, soldiers, diplomats, merchants and women all shaped the Republic and experienced its institutions differently.

A modern history guide should also confront the Republic's constitutional protection of slavery, continued conflict and dispossession affecting Native peoples, and political restrictions that determined who could exercise full citizenship.

The Republic is more interesting when it is treated as a real 19th-century nation with contradictions and unresolved problems, not simply as a romantic intermission between revolution and statehood.

## A simple Republic of Texas timeline

- **March 2, 1836:** Declaration of Independence adopted at Washington-on-the-Brazos
- **April 21, 1836:** Texian victory at San Jacinto
- **September 1836:** Texas voters support annexation
- **March 3, 1837:** United States recognizes the Republic of Texas
- **1838:** Texas withdraws its first annexation offer
- **1844:** U.S. Senate rejects an annexation treaty
- **March 1, 1845:** President John Tyler signs the U.S. annexation resolution
- **July 1845:** Texas convention accepts the U.S. offer
- **October 1845:** Texas voters approve annexation/state constitution
- **December 29, 1845:** Texas officially becomes the 28th state
- **February 19, 1846:** Republic government formally transfers to the new state government
