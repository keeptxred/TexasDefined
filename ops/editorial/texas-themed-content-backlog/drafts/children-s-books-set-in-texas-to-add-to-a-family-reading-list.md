# Children’s Books Set in Texas to Add to a Family Reading List

A Texas reading list is more useful when it does not confuse three different things: books **set in Texas**, books **about Texas history or nature**, and books **written by Texas authors**. All three can belong on a family shelf, but they are not the same category.

This draft focuses first on stories with a clear Texas setting or Texas-centered subject, then points families toward current librarian-curated lists for newer titles.

## 1. *The Legend of the Bluebonnet* — Tomie dePaola

This widely read picture book retells a story connected to the Texas bluebonnet and Comanche tradition.

Because it draws on Indigenous cultural material, publication copy should avoid presenting the book as a literal primary-source account of Comanche history. It works best as a springboard for discussing storytelling, state symbols and the difference between legend and documented history.

Pair it with TexasDefined’s bluebonnet and wildflower coverage.

## 2. *Bluebonnet at Dinosaur Valley State Park* — Mary Brooke Casad

The Texas State Library’s children’s reading resources have highlighted this book, which follows the armadillo character Bluebonnet through Dinosaur Valley State Park.

It provides an easy bridge from reading into state parks, dinosaur tracks and Texas geology.

## 3. *The Evolution of Calpurnia Tate* — Jacqueline Kelly

Set in Central Texas around the turn of the twentieth century, the novel follows a young girl developing an interest in natural history and scientific observation.

It can connect family reading with Texas ecology, history of science and changing expectations for girls and women.

## 4. *Holes* — Louis Sachar

The novel’s fictional Camp Green Lake is set in Texas and uses the state’s heat, dryness and landscape as important parts of the story.

Families should treat the setting as fiction rather than a geography lesson, but it creates useful discussion about how authors turn real regional features into imagined places.

## 5. *A Paradise Called Texas* — Janice Jordan Shefelman

This historical novel follows a German immigrant family coming to Texas in the nineteenth century.

It can support conversations about immigration, settlement, hardship and the German-Texan communities that later shaped parts of Central Texas.

Historical-fiction details should be distinguished from documented history when the article links to deeper Texas history pages.

## 6. *The Underneath* — Kathi Appelt

This atmospheric novel is rooted in East Texas woods and bayous.

Its setting can introduce older readers to the Piney Woods as a literary landscape before sending them into TexasDefined’s factual regional and nature guides.

## 7. *Ambassador of Nowhere, Texas* — Kimberly Willis Holt

This middle-grade book connects family history, memory and a small Texas community.

It also appears in Texas youth-reading programs and offers a contemporary path into discussing how Texas identity differs from one town and generation to another.

## 8. *Hank the Cowdog* series — John R. Erickson

The long-running series is rooted in ranch life on the Texas High Plains and has introduced generations of children to a highly comic version of rural Texas.

In 2026, the Texas Center for the Book selected *Hank the Cowdog #83: The Case of the Poison Toad* as the Texas Great Read youth selection.

The series is especially useful for connecting reading to ranching, Panhandle geography, working animals and rural culture.

## 9. Texas state-park and nature books

TSLAC and TPWD maintain children’s and educator reading resources tied to state parks, wildlife and conservation.

Rather than hard-code one permanent list, a live version should include a small rotating box such as **“Current Texas nature books for kids”** sourced from those institutional lists.

That makes the page refreshable without replacing its evergreen core.

## 10. Current Texas librarian reading lists

Families looking for newer middle-grade and young-adult books should use current Texas librarian-curated programs rather than relying only on classics.

Useful programs include:

- Spirit of Texas Reading Program;
- Lone Star Reading List;
- TAYSHAS for high-school readers;
- Texas Bluebonnet Award lists;
- Texas Great Read selections.

Not every title on those lists is set in Texas. The page should label whether a recommendation qualifies because of setting, Texas authorship or inclusion on a Texas reading program.

## Build the list by age and purpose

A better family reading guide can group books by what a reader wants:

### Picture books and read-alouds
Use state symbols, animals, parks and legends to build familiarity with Texas.

### Middle grade
Look for stories about small towns, ranches, parks, family history and Texas landscapes.

### Young adult
Use current librarian lists for more recent fiction and nonfiction connected to Texas authors and themes.

### History and nature companions
Pair fiction with age-appropriate nonfiction from libraries, museums, TPWD and TSLAC.

## Questions parents can ask while reading

Instead of treating “Texas books” as trivia, ask:

- What part of Texas is this story using?
- Is the setting real, fictional or a mixture?
- What historical details can we verify?
- Whose perspective is centered?
- What parts of Texas culture are missing?
- How does the landscape affect the story?
- Is a legend being presented as history?
