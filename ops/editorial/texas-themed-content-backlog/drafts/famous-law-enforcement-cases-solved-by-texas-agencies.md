# Famous Law-Enforcement Cases Solved by Texas Agencies

A Texas case-history article can easily become sensational or inaccurate. This guide should focus on closed, well-documented cases where the investigative role of a Texas agency can be established from court records, agency histories or major archival reporting.

## What qualifies

- The case is closed or historically settled enough for responsible summary.
- The relevant agency’s role is documented.
- The article can distinguish investigation, arrest, prosecution and conviction rather than crediting one agency with the entire process.
- Victims are treated as people, not plot devices.

## Agency types to explain

### Texas Rangers / DPS
Rangers have participated in major homicide, corruption, fugitive and historical investigations, but institutional claims require precise sourcing.

### Local police and sheriff’s offices
Many famous Texas cases were primarily local investigations with state or federal assistance.

### Attorney General and specialized state units
Some financial, cyber, trafficking or public-integrity cases involve specialized jurisdiction; do not blur civil and criminal roles.

### Federal partnerships
FBI, ATF, DEA, U.S. Marshals and other federal agencies often work with Texas agencies. Attribution should reflect joint operations accurately.

## Editorial safeguards

Do not include unsolved active cases as “solved.” Avoid graphic crime details that do not add explanatory value. Correctly distinguish allegation, charge, plea, conviction, acquittal and exoneration.

## Internal-link plan

Link to Texas Rangers history, state institutions, county/city history and museum/archive content where the case has a strong public-history connection.

## Source notes

Use final court opinions, indictments/judgments where public, agency case histories, DOJ/FBI records and reputable archival journalism. Recheck later exonerations, overturned convictions or disputed forensic methods.

## Image guidance

Use non-graphic rights-cleared courthouse, agency-history or archival location imagery. Avoid victims’ private photographs and sensational evidence images.
