# 15 Women Who Shaped Texas History

Texas history is often told through presidents, generals, cattlemen, oilmen and politicians. That leaves out women whose influence ran through ranching, preservation, aviation, journalism, civil rights, military service, education, philanthropy and public office.

A useful list should also resist another trap: pretending all influence looks the same. Some of these women held formal power. Others built institutions, organized communities, protected historic places, challenged segregation or created economic power in periods when law and custom sharply limited women's public roles.

These fifteen are not a ranking. They are entry points into different parts of Texas history.

## 1. Rosa María Hinojosa de Ballí — South Texas ranching and landholding

Rosa María Hinojosa de Ballí lived before Texas was a republic or a U.S. state. Humanities Texas identifies her as the first "cattle queen" of Texas.

After inheriting tens of thousands of acres in 1790, she expanded her family's ranching interests dramatically in the lower Rio Grande region. Humanities Texas notes that by her death in 1803 she controlled more than a million acres of ranch land.

Her story belongs in Texas ranching history because it pushes the timeline back into the Spanish colonial period and shows that women could exercise substantial economic power despite the era's restrictions.

## 2. Susanna Dickinson — witness to the Alamo and revolution

Susanna Dickinson survived the Battle of the Alamo with her young daughter and carried news of the defeat eastward during the Texas Revolution.

Her historical importance is not that she became a battlefield commander. It is that her testimony became part of how later generations understood the Alamo and the crisis facing Texian forces in March 1836.

Her story also helps readers see civilians — especially women and children — inside a revolution often told almost entirely through soldiers.

## 3. Angelina Eberly — the cannon shot that helped keep the archives in Austin

Angelina Eberly was an Austin innkeeper whose name became inseparable from the Archives War.

In December 1842, when officials attempted to remove Republic government records from Austin, Eberly fired a six-pound cannon to alert residents. The Texas State Historical Association says the action helped trigger the local response that ultimately returned the archives to Austin.

The episode made her a symbol of Austin's fight to retain its status as the seat of government.

## 4. Adina de Zavala — preservation before preservation was fashionable

Adina de Zavala, granddaughter of Republic-era leader Lorenzo de Zavala, became one of the state's early historic-preservation activists.

She worked to preserve Spanish mission and Texas Revolution sites, including structures associated with the Alamo. Her methods and priorities sometimes brought her into conflict with other preservationists, which is part of the history rather than a reason to flatten it.

De Zavala's larger legacy is the idea that Texas history needed physical places, not only stories.

## 5. Clara Driscoll — preservation, philanthropy and the Alamo

Clara Driscoll is often called the "Savior of the Alamo" because of her role in helping secure and preserve Alamo property in the early 20th century.

Her work overlapped and at times clashed with Adina de Zavala's preservation vision. A good article should present both women rather than turning one into the sole hero of a complicated preservation campaign.

Driscoll later became a major philanthropist and civic figure, extending her influence far beyond the Alamo.

## 6. Minnie Fisher Cunningham — Texas suffrage organizer

Minnie Fisher Cunningham was one of the most important Texas leaders in the movement for women's voting rights. She helped build statewide organization around suffrage and later carried that work into national politics and reform.

Her story connects Texas directly to the struggle that culminated in the Nineteenth Amendment, while also showing that voting-rights gains were unequal and did not erase racial barriers to the ballot.

## 7. Miriam "Ma" Ferguson — the first woman governor of Texas

Miriam Ferguson became the first woman to serve as governor of Texas. She won election in 1924 and later returned for a second nonconsecutive term.

Her career cannot be separated from that of her husband, former governor James Ferguson, whose impeachment and political organization shaped her rise. That makes her more interesting historically, not less: Ferguson's governorship reveals how gender, family political machines and reform battles intersected in early 20th-century Texas.

## 8. Bessie Coleman — aviation and refusal to accept segregation

Born in northeast Texas in 1892, Bessie Coleman became a pioneering Black and Native American aviator after American flight schools refused her opportunities because of race and gender.

Humanities Texas describes Coleman as the world's first female African American aviator. She built a career through exhibition flying and used her visibility to encourage Black participation in aviation while refusing to support segregated events.

Her Texas connection belongs in both civil-rights and aviation history.

## 9. Oveta Culp Hobby — military organization and federal leadership

Oveta Culp Hobby moved from Texas government and publishing into national wartime leadership. During World War II she helped organize the Women's Army Auxiliary Corps and became its first director.

Humanities Texas notes that more than 150,000 women served through the organization and that Hobby received the Army Distinguished Service Medal. In 1953, President Dwight Eisenhower selected her to lead the newly created Department of Health, Education, and Welfare.

Her career links Texas political life, media, women's military service and federal administration.

## 10. Ima Hogg — arts, mental health and preservation philanthropy

Ima Hogg used inherited wealth and organizational skill to shape Texas cultural institutions. Humanities Texas highlights her philanthropy, arts patronage and preservation of historic properties.

Her work touched the Houston Symphony, mental-health advocacy, museums, decorative arts and preserved Texas places including properties later transferred for public benefit.

The important story is not her unusual name. It is the institution-building she financed and sustained.

## 11. Emma Tenayuca — labor organizing in San Antonio

Emma Tenayuca emerged as a major Mexican American labor organizer in San Antonio during the 1930s. She became closely associated with the pecan shellers' strike of 1938, one of the most important labor conflicts in Texas history.

Her politics were controversial in her lifetime and should be presented accurately, including her Communist Party involvement rather than sanitized away. Her importance lies in organizing workers — many of them Mexican American women — around wages, working conditions and political voice.

## 12. Juanita Craft — civil rights from Dallas neighborhoods to City Hall

Juanita Craft spent decades organizing through the NAACP and challenging segregation in Dallas and across Texas.

Her activism included youth organizing, voter work and campaigns against segregated public accommodations and institutions. She later served on the Dallas City Council, carrying a lifetime of grassroots organizing into formal local government.

Craft's story is especially useful for linking statewide civil-rights history to specific Dallas neighborhoods and sites.

## 13. Barbara Jordan — Texas Senate, Congress and constitutional leadership

Barbara Jordan was elected to the Texas Senate in 1966, becoming the first African American woman elected to that body. In 1972 she won a seat in the U.S. House, becoming the first African American woman from a southern state to serve there.

Her national reputation grew through her command of constitutional argument, including during the Watergate era. Yet her career remained deeply rooted in Houston's Fifth Ward and Texas politics.

Jordan is essential to both Texas political history and the broader story of representation in American government.

## 14. Sarah T. Hughes — law, public service and a defining national photograph

Sarah T. Hughes built a long Texas legal and political career before becoming a federal judge. She is nationally remembered as the judge who administered the presidential oath to Lyndon B. Johnson aboard Air Force One in Dallas on November 22, 1963.

That moment should not swallow the rest of her career. Hughes was also a legislator, judge and advocate whose path reflected the slow expansion of professional opportunities for women in Texas law and politics.

## 15. Ann Richards — modern Texas political identity

Ann Richards served as Texas state treasurer before winning the governorship in 1990. Her public style, national visibility and administration made her one of the most recognizable Texas political figures of the late 20th century.

Her place on this list is not an endorsement of her policies. It is recognition that her election, communication style and role as a woman at the top of Texas government became part of the state's modern political history.

## The larger story: influence without one definition

These women shaped Texas in radically different ways:

- **land and ranching:** Rosa María Hinojosa de Ballí
- **revolutionary memory:** Susanna Dickinson
- **capital and preservation:** Angelina Eberly, Adina de Zavala, Clara Driscoll
- **voting rights:** Minnie Fisher Cunningham
- **state government:** Miriam Ferguson, Ann Richards
- **aviation and equal opportunity:** Bessie Coleman
- **military/public administration:** Oveta Culp Hobby
- **arts and philanthropy:** Ima Hogg
- **labor rights:** Emma Tenayuca
- **civil rights:** Juanita Craft, Barbara Jordan
- **law and judiciary:** Sarah T. Hughes

A deeper Texas history section should eventually give many of them individual pages and connect their stories to museums, historic sites, counties, schools, archives and primary documents.
