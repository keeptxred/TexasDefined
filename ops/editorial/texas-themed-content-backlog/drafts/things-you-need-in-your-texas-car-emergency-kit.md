# 15 Things to Keep in a Texas Car Emergency Kit

A Texas vehicle kit has to handle more than one stereotype. Summer heat can be dangerous, but Texas also gets flash floods, severe thunderstorms, wildfire smoke, hurricanes, ice and hard freezes. Long rural distances can turn a minor breakdown into a serious problem.

This guide should complement—not replace—official emergency instructions. Readers should still follow law-enforcement, TxDOT, local emergency-management and weather warnings.

## 1. Drinking water

Ready.gov includes water in basic emergency supplies. In a vehicle, the amount should reflect the people traveling, trip length and season.

Do not leave questionable or damaged containers in extreme heat indefinitely. Rotate supplies routinely.

## 2. Shelf-stable food

Pack simple nonperishable food that can be eaten without cooking. Heat-tolerant packaging and periodic replacement matter in a Texas car.

## 3. Phone charging cable and power bank

A charged phone can provide maps, alerts and emergency communication. A standalone power bank helps when the vehicle battery or USB system fails.

Recharge it on a regular schedule rather than assuming it is full months later.

## 4. Flashlight or headlamp

A headlamp leaves both hands free during a nighttime tire change or roadside inspection. Include spare batteries if the light uses replaceable cells.

## 5. First-aid kit

Use a commercially complete kit or assemble one appropriate to household needs. Replace expired items and medicines.

This article should not prescribe medication or medical treatment.

## 6. Reflective warning equipment

High-visibility triangles or other lawful warning devices can make a stopped vehicle more visible. Placement depends on road conditions and safety.

Never stand in a traffic lane to deploy equipment.

## 7. Jumper cables or a maintained jump pack

Texas heat is hard on vehicle batteries. A jump pack can be useful when another vehicle is not available, but it has to be charged and stored according to the manufacturer’s temperature guidance.

## 8. Tire tools that actually fit the vehicle

Confirm the vehicle has a usable spare or manufacturer-provided repair system, jack, lug wrench and wheel-lock key if applicable.

A kit is useless if the driver discovers roadside that the spare is flat or the wheel key is at home.

## 9. Basic tire-pressure gauge and inflator

Temperature shifts and long highway trips can expose marginal tire pressure. A compact inflator may help with a slow leak but does not make a damaged tire safe to drive on.

## 10. Paper map or offline map backup

Ready.gov includes local maps in emergency supplies. Remote West Texas and disaster conditions can make mobile data unreliable.

Download offline maps before rural trips and keep a simple paper backup for major routes.

## 11. Seasonal clothing and sun protection

A summer kit can include a hat, lightweight long sleeves and sunscreen. Winter travel may require a blanket, gloves and warm layers.

Texas weather can change sharply across regions, so review the kit by season.

## 12. Work gloves and basic tools

Gloves protect hands when handling a hot engine compartment, tire tools or roadside debris. Keep tool selection modest and relevant to repairs the driver can safely perform.

## 13. Emergency contact and roadside-assistance information

Store insurer, roadside-assistance and important contact details somewhere accessible even if the phone is dead or locked.

## 14. Vehicle-specific emergency supplies

Some vehicles require special tow points, EV charging planning, tire-sealant systems or other manufacturer-specific procedures. Review the owner’s manual.

For EVs, route and charging planning can be especially important in remote areas or during widespread outages.

## 15. A weather-and-route check before the trip

The best emergency item is avoiding a known hazard. Check TxDOT road conditions, National Weather Service warnings and local emergency information before long-distance travel.

Never drive around barricades or into floodwater. “Turn Around, Don’t Drown” is especially relevant in Texas flash-flood country.

## Build the kit by Texas hazard

### Extreme heat

Prioritize water, shade/sun protection, communication and avoiding prolonged roadside exposure.

### Winter weather

Add warm clothing, blanket, scraper as appropriate and extra food/water. Do not assume a Texas road will remain passable because snow accumulation is light; ice can be more disruptive.

### Hurricane/coastal evacuation

Keep fuel/charge planning, maps, medications and documents ready before evacuation traffic peaks.

### Remote West Texas travel

Increase water, fuel/charging margin and offline navigation. Service gaps can be long.
