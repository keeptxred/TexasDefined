# Essentials for a Texas Backyard Barbecue Menu

A Texas backyard barbecue does not need to imitate a commercial pit room. The strongest menu is built around one or two smoked or grilled anchors, a few sides that can survive the weather, cold drinks and enough planning that the cook is not handling raw meat, cooked food and guests at the same time.

This guide treats “Texas barbecue” as a family of regional traditions rather than one mandatory menu.

## 1. Pick one main meat you can execute well

Brisket is iconic, but it is not mandatory. A backyard cookout can center on:

- brisket;
- pork ribs;
- beef ribs;
- sausage;
- chicken;
- turkey;
- pork shoulder;
- burgers or hot links for a shorter cook.

The better rule is **do fewer proteins well** rather than attempting a restaurant-sized spread.

## 2. Add sausage for flexibility

Texas barbecue traditions have deep Central European and meat-market influences, and sausage remains a practical backyard addition because it can feed a crowd without requiring another all-day cook.

Use local or regional sausage if that matters to the menu, but do not claim a brand is “Texas-made” without verifying where it is actually produced.

## 3. Keep sliced bread, pickles and onions simple

Butcher-paper barbecue service often includes white bread, sliced onions, pickles and jalapeños.

These are useful because they add acidity, crunch and an easy way for guests to build sandwiches without another cooked side.

## 4. Choose beans based on the barbecue table, not the chili debate

Beans are common barbecue sides even though competition-style Texas chili has a famous no-beans tradition.

Do not confuse the two topics. Pinto beans, borracho-style beans or smoked beans can fit naturally beside barbecue.

## 5. Potato salad gives the menu a cold, filling side

Potato salad appears in many Texas barbecue settings, but recipes vary widely.

Because it is perishable, keep it cold until serving and return leftovers to safe refrigeration promptly. Outdoor food-safety rules matter more in Texas heat than arguing over mustard versus mayonnaise.

## 6. Coleslaw adds crunch and acidity

A vinegar-forward or creamy slaw can balance rich meat. If using a dairy- or mayonnaise-based version, treat it as a chilled perishable food.

## 7. Macaroni and cheese is optional, not a Texas authenticity test

Modern barbecue menus often include mac and cheese. It can be popular with children and guests who want a substantial meat-free side.

Its presence does not make the meal more or less “authentic.”

## 8. Include one fresh or vegetable side

A backyard menu benefits from something lighter:

- tomato-cucumber salad;
- grilled corn;
- watermelon;
- green salad;
- grilled squash;
- pico de gallo.

That keeps the spread from becoming six versions of starch plus meat.

## 9. Sauce can be offered without drowning the meat

Texas barbecue sauce traditions vary by region and pit style. Put sauce on the side and let guests decide.

A statewide article should not turn one Central Texas convention into a universal rule for every Texas barbecue tradition.

## 10. Jalapeños and salsa make sense on many Texas tables

Fresh or pickled jalapeños, salsa and pico de gallo can connect the barbecue spread to broader Texas food traditions.

Keep raw produce clean and chilled where appropriate.

## 11. Dessert should be easy to serve outdoors

Good options include:

- cobbler;
- sheet cake;
- cookies;
- banana pudding kept cold;
- fruit;
- ice cream served immediately from proper cold storage.

Avoid leaving dairy-heavy desserts on a hot patio for extended periods.

## 12. Build a drink station away from the raw-food prep area

Keeping beverages separate reduces traffic around the pit and helps keep guests away from knives, hot surfaces and raw meat.

A separate beverage cooler also means the food cooler is opened less often.

## 13. Make food safety part of the menu plan

Texas A&M AgriLife Extension recommends keeping raw meat, poultry and seafood cold, separating raw foods from ready-to-eat foods, marinating in the refrigerator and keeping coolers shaded and closed as much as possible.

Use a food thermometer rather than color alone to judge doneness, and use current USDA/AgriLife temperatures for the specific protein before publication.

## 14. Plan for the Texas heat

AgriLife’s 2026 family-health guidance emphasizes keeping cold foods cold and hot foods hot, with shorter room-temperature limits when outdoor temperatures exceed 90°F.

That makes serving order important. Bring cold sides out near mealtime instead of setting everything on a table while the pit finishes.

## 15. The best backyard menu is one you can serve safely and enjoy

A cookout should not require the host to spend the entire gathering slicing five meats and rescuing warm side dishes.

A practical Texas menu might be:

- brisket or ribs;
- sausage;
- beans;
- potato salad or slaw;
- pickles/onions/bread;
- one fresh side;
- cobbler or sheet cake.

That is enough variety to feel generous without turning a backyard into a commercial kitchen.
