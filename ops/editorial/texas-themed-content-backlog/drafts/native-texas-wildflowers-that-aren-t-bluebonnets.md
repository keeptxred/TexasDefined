# 15 Native Texas Wildflowers That Aren't Bluebonnets

Bluebonnets get the license plates, the family photos and most of the spring headlines. They are only one piece of the Texas wildflower season.

Across prairies, roadsides, sandy post-oak country, Hill Country limestone and East Texas openings, other native flowers add red, yellow, pink, purple and white long after the bluebonnets begin to fade. Texas Parks and Wildlife and Texas A&M AgriLife plant lists make the larger point clear: the right species depends on region, soil, moisture and season.

This is not a "plant these anywhere in Texas" list. It is a field-guide-style introduction to flowers Texans can learn to recognize and then research for their own region.

## 1. Indian paintbrush — Castilleja indivisa

Indian paintbrush is the classic bluebonnet companion, often creating red-orange bands through the same spring fields. What looks like the flower is largely colorful bract tissue around much smaller flowers.

Because paintbrush can be partially parasitic on neighboring plant roots, it behaves differently from a simple bedding annual. Enjoy it in the landscape first; research local propagation guidance before trying to recreate a roadside field at home.

## 2. Indian blanket / firewheel — Gaillardia pulchella

Red and yellow daisy-like petals make Indian blanket one of the easiest Texas wildflowers to recognize. TPWD plant guidance lists it for open grassy areas, prairies, meadows and disturbed sites across a range of soils.

Its season can extend well beyond the earliest spring bloom, helping carry roadside color toward summer.

## 3. Winecup — Callirhoe involucrata

Winecups form low mats with deep magenta cup-shaped flowers. They are especially striking when spilling across open ground rather than standing upright like a typical roadside daisy.

They are a good reminder to look down: some of Texas's best wildflowers hug the soil instead of creating tall fields.

## 4. Pink evening primrose — Oenothera speciosa

Pink evening primrose can create broad pale-pink patches along roadsides and open ground. The flowers often fade toward white as they age, and colonies can spread aggressively where conditions suit them.

The common roadside name "buttercup" is used loosely in Texas, but evening primrose is not a true buttercup.

## 5. Texas bluebell — Eustoma species

Texas bluebells produce elegant purple-to-blue flowers and favor different moisture conditions than many dry-prairie species. TPWD regional plant lists include Texas bluebell in moist prairie settings.

Because wild populations can be locally sensitive, photograph flowers where they grow rather than digging or collecting them.

## 6. American basketflower — Centaurea americana

Basketflower is a tall prairie annual with large lavender-pink flower heads and distinctive basket-like bracts beneath the bloom.

It can look almost too ornamental to be native, which makes it especially useful in prairie-restoration and native-garden education.

## 7. Lemon beebalm / horsemint — Monarda citriodora

Horsemint produces stacked purple-pink flower whorls and a strong scent when foliage is brushed. TPWD and native-pollinator lists include Monarda species as useful nectar plants.

It often becomes more noticeable after the earliest spring flowers fade.

## 8. Prairie verbena — Glandularia species

Low purple verbena can appear in open sunny areas and along roadsides, sometimes forming bright patches close to the ground.

Several verbena species occur in Texas, so a statewide article should avoid pretending every purple roadside cluster is the same species without a closer identification.

## 9. Mexican hat — Ratibida columnifera

Mexican hat gets its name from the tall central cone surrounded by drooping petals, often yellow, red or a mix of both.

It is common in prairie and roadside settings and fits naturally into Texas pollinator-garden content.

## 10. Black-eyed Susan — Rudbeckia hirta

Golden petals around a dark center make black-eyed Susan familiar even to people who do not think of themselves as wildflower watchers. TPWD pollinator guidance includes the species in native prairie plant lists.

It tends to appear later than peak bluebonnet season, extending the visual calendar.

## 11. Coreopsis — Coreopsis species

Texas has multiple coreopsis species, including forms with bright yellow flowers and others with red-brown centers. TPWD regional recommendations list Coreopsis tinctoria in moist sandy sites, while AgriLife native-plant material includes other native coreopsis choices.

Use the genus as an entry point, then identify the local species rather than forcing one statewide label.

## 12. Standing cypress — Ipomopsis rubra

Standing cypress sends up tall spikes of vivid red tubular flowers. It is especially dramatic because its vertical form looks completely different from a carpet of bluebonnets or daisies.

TPWD has documented it among native wildflowers in East Texas/post-oak habitats, and hummingbirds are naturally drawn to the tubular blooms.

## 13. Greenthread — Thelesperma filifolium

Greenthread is a fine-leaved yellow wildflower common in open sunny settings across parts of Texas. The delicate foliage gives it a lighter look than larger prairie daisies.

It is one of those plants travelers may pass for years before learning the name.

## 14. Blazing star / gayfeather — Liatris species

Liatris produces tall spikes of purple flowers that can become magnets for pollinators. Texas A&M AgriLife native-plant materials include prairie blazing star and other Liatris species.

These are more associated with prairie and warm-season color than the classic March bluebonnet photo, which makes them valuable for showing that Texas wildflower season is not a single spring weekend.

## 15. Purple coneflower and its Texas relatives — Echinacea species

Coneflowers bring purple petals and raised central disks to prairie and native-garden settings. AgriLife regional plant-selection materials include native coneflower options, but species suitability varies by region.

For landscaping, use a local native-plant source or county Extension recommendation rather than buying a plant simply because the tag says "Texas native."

## Wildflower season is bigger than bluebonnet season

A useful Texas wildflower calendar starts before peak bluebonnets and continues through summer and fall. Species respond differently to winter cold, spring rain, soil moisture and geography.

That means a disappointing bluebonnet year can still produce excellent paintbrush, firewheel, primrose, prairie flowers or later-season blooms. Travelers should watch current regional reports rather than treating one famous species as the whole season.

## Don't pick, trample or trespass for the photo

The legal and ecological questions around wildflower picking can be more complicated than social media suggests. The simplest good behavior is universal:

- stay off private property unless you have permission
- do not cross fences or block gates
- use safe, legal parking
- avoid flattening flowers for portraits
- follow park/site rules
- never collect protected or rare plants
- leave enough space around roads and traffic

A field full of flowers is habitat, not a studio set.

## Want these flowers in your yard?

Start with your county, not the statewide list. Texas A&M AgriLife's plant resources and native-plant lists can help match species to soil, rainfall, sunlight and region.

The TexasDefined native-plant cluster should eventually let readers move from "what flower is that?" to "will it actually thrive where I live?"
