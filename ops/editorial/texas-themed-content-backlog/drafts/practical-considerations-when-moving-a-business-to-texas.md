# 15 Practical Considerations When Moving a Business to Texas

Moving a company to Texas can mean several very different things: physically relocating an existing business, registering an out-of-state entity to transact business here, forming a new Texas entity, moving employees, opening a second location, or changing tax and operational footprints without changing the entity's state of formation.

Those paths do not have the same legal or tax consequences. This is a practical research checklist, not legal or tax advice. Businesses should verify current requirements with the Texas Secretary of State, Comptroller, Texas Workforce Commission, licensing agencies and qualified advisers before acting.

## 1. Decide whether you are moving the entity or only the operations

An existing company formed in another state can sometimes remain organized there and register as a foreign entity in Texas. Another business may choose a merger, conversion, new Texas entity or other restructuring.

Do not treat “move to Texas” as a filing instruction. Start with the legal structure and desired end state.

## 2. Determine whether the existing entity must register to transact business in Texas

The Texas Secretary of State requires specified foreign filing entities to register if they are “transacting business” in Texas. Texas law lists activities that do not, by themselves, constitute transacting business, and the Secretary of State does not make individualized legal determinations for companies.

A business with employees, an office or regular operations in Texas should evaluate registration early rather than discovering the issue after contracts, litigation or licensing problems arise.

## 3. Do not confuse Secretary of State registration with tax nexus

The Secretary of State notes that the threshold for Texas tax nexus can be different from the threshold for entity registration.

A company can therefore have a Texas tax obligation even when a corporate-law registration analysis is less obvious. Run the tax analysis separately.

## 4. Maintain a Texas registered agent and registered office when required

Domestic Texas filing entities and foreign filing entities registered in Texas must maintain a registered agent and registered office in Texas.

The registered office must be a physical Texas address where the agent can receive service during business hours. The entity cannot simply name itself as its own registered agent.

## 5. Check entity-name availability and assumed-name issues

A name used elsewhere may not be available for a Texas filing or foreign registration. Trademark rights and Secretary of State name availability are also not the same thing.

Check entity records, assumed-name requirements and trademark implications before rebranding signs, websites, contracts and bank accounts.

## 6. Understand Texas franchise-tax and reporting obligations

Texas imposes a franchise tax on many taxable entities and requires associated information reports or ownership-information filings depending on entity type and current law.

Thresholds, rates, due dates and reporting rules change. Link readers to the Comptroller's current franchise-tax pages instead of freezing a threshold into evergreen copy.

## 7. Determine whether you need a Texas sales-tax permit

Businesses selling taxable goods or services may need to obtain a Texas sales-and-use-tax permit and collect/remit tax.

Whether a specific product or service is taxable can be surprisingly technical. E-commerce businesses also need to consider physical and economic nexus rules.

## 8. Register for employer obligations before payroll surprises you

A business moving employees to Texas or hiring here should review Texas Workforce Commission employer registration, unemployment-tax rules and payroll reporting.

Also account for federal payroll obligations and multi-state issues when some workers remain outside Texas.

## 9. Check industry licenses before signing a lease

Texas does not have one universal state business license, but many occupations and industries are licensed or regulated by specific state agencies, boards, cities or counties.

A restaurant, child-care facility, contractor, professional practice, transportation company, financial business and industrial operation can each have very different requirements.

Map licensing to the actual activity before committing to a location.

## 10. Choose the location based on operations, not the slogan

Texas is enormous. A warehouse near AllianceTexas, a refinery supplier near Houston, an aerospace manufacturer in North Texas, a software office in Austin and a cross-border logistics company in Laredo need different infrastructure.

Compare:

- labor pool and wage competition;
- customer/vendor proximity;
- interstate and freight access;
- airport/port/rail access;
- electricity and utility capacity;
- water needs;
- broadband;
- local taxes and incentives;
- permitting/zoning;
- housing and commute patterns for employees.

## 11. Model property tax and special-district costs

No individual state income tax does not mean a business location has low total local costs. Real and business personal property taxation, sales taxes, utility costs and local assessments can affect the economics.

For owned real estate, research the exact appraisal district and taxing units. For leased space, understand which operating expenses and tax increases pass through under the lease.

## 12. Electricity arrangements vary across Texas

Some Texas locations operate in competitive retail electricity areas; others are served by municipal utilities or electric cooperatives. Large commercial and industrial loads may also face issues far beyond a residential rate comparison.

Identify the utility territory and capacity needs before final site selection.

## 13. Incentives should come after the base economics work

State and local economic-development incentives can matter, but a business should first establish that the location works without a speculative incentive package.

Evaluate clawbacks, job/investment commitments, reporting obligations, timing and what happens if business conditions change.

## 14. Plan the people move separately from the company move

Employees may care more about housing, schools, insurance, commuting and spouse employment than corporate tax structure.

A relocation package should therefore connect business-site selection with the same city, county, cost-of-living and moving tools used by households.

## 15. Build a post-move compliance calendar

The move is not finished when the Secretary of State filing is accepted.

Create a calendar covering:

- franchise-tax/reporting deadlines;
- sales-tax filings;
- unemployment/payroll reporting;
- registered-agent maintenance;
- licenses and renewals;
- local permits;
- assumed names;
- property-tax renditions where applicable;
- annual reports in the old state until the old obligations are properly ended;
- contract, insurance and banking updates.

That turns a relocation into an operating system rather than a one-time filing project.
