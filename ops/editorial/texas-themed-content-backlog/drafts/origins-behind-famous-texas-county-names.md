# 15 Origins Behind Famous Texas County Names

Texas has 254 counties, and their names form a compressed index of the state’s history. Some honor presidents, revolutionaries and soldiers. Others preserve Spanish geography, early settlers or figures whose reputations look different when viewed from the twenty-first century.

A useful county-name guide should therefore do more than say who a county was named for. It should also explain uncertainty, changing historical interpretation and the difference between a county’s namesake and the modern communities inside it.

## 1. Travis County — William Barret Travis

Travis County was named for William Barret Travis, commander of the Texian garrison at the Alamo in 1836. Modern Austin makes the county name feel contemporary, but its origin reaches directly into Texas Revolution memory.

## 2. Harris County — John Richardson Harris

Harris County honors John Richardson Harris, an early settler and founder of Harrisburg. The county’s name links modern Houston to an older settlement at the head of Buffalo Bayou that played an important role in early Texas transportation and government.

## 3. Bexar County — San Fernando de Béxar

Bexar carries a Spanish colonial name rather than an Anglo-Texan surname. San Antonio de Béxar was tied to the presidio, villa and mission complex that developed in Spanish Texas.

This is a good reminder that Texas county names do not begin with the Republic era.

## 4. Galveston County — Bernardo de Gálvez

Galveston takes its name from Bernardo de Gálvez, the Spanish military leader and colonial governor whose name was applied to Galveston Bay before the modern city and county developed.

The name connects the Gulf Coast to Spanish imperial history and the American Revolution.

## 5. Houston County — Sam Houston

Houston County was named for Sam Houston, but it is not the county containing the City of Houston. Houston County lies in East Texas, with Crockett as its county seat.

That distinction makes it especially useful in a “Texas names can fool you” sidebar.

## 6. Austin County — Stephen F. Austin

Austin County honors Stephen F. Austin and sits in the region of his early colony. Like Houston County, it is not the county containing the city that shares the name; Austin is in Travis County.

## 7. Bowie County — James Bowie

Bowie County honors James Bowie, whose name became closely associated with the Alamo and the famous knife tradition.

A deeper profile should separate documented biography from later legend.

## 8. Crockett County — Davy Crockett

Crockett County in West Texas was named for David “Davy” Crockett, the Tennessee politician and frontiersman who died at the Alamo.

The county’s geography is far removed from the Alamo, showing how Revolution-era commemoration spread across later-organized counties.

## 9. Lamar County — Mirabeau B. Lamar

Lamar County honors Mirabeau B. Lamar, president of the Republic of Texas. His administration is remembered for education advocacy and expansionist policies, but also for violent policies toward Native peoples.

Any future full biography should present both parts rather than a one-line heroic summary.

## 10. Tarrant County — Edward H. Tarrant

Tarrant County was named for Edward H. Tarrant, a Republic-era militia leader and politician. Fort Worth later became the dominant city in the county.

Because Tarrant’s military activity intersected with violent frontier conflict, a deeper page should use modern historical scholarship rather than old commemorative language alone.

## 11. Collin County — Collin McKinney

Collin County honors Collin McKinney, a signer of the Texas Declaration of Independence and political figure in the Republic and early statehood periods.

The City of McKinney, the county seat, also carries his surname, making this one of the clearest county-seat/name pairings.

## 12. Denton County — John B. Denton

Denton County and the City of Denton were named for John B. Denton, a preacher, lawyer and militia participant who died during a frontier expedition.

As with other frontier namesakes, the modern article should describe the conflict context carefully rather than relying only on nineteenth-century commemorative accounts.

## 13. El Paso County — the pass

El Paso comes from Spanish geographic language associated with the pass through the mountains and the broader Paso del Norte region.

Unlike counties named for individuals, this name preserves geography and a much older cross-border regional identity.

## 14. Dallas County — an uncertain namesake

Dallas County’s exact namesake is historically uncertain. Various explanations have been proposed, but researchers have not established one definitive answer accepted by everyone.

That uncertainty is itself valuable content. A good history site should say “we do not know for sure” when the record does not support certainty.

## 15. Washington County — George Washington

Washington County honors George Washington. Its historical significance in Texas goes far beyond the name because Washington-on-the-Brazos was where delegates met in 1836 and adopted the Texas Declaration of Independence.

The county-name entry should link directly into the Independence Day and Republic-era cluster.
