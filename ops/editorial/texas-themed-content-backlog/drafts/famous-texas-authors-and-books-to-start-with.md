# Texas Authors and Books to Start With

Texas literature is too broad to reduce to Westerns. The state’s writers have worked in fiction, memoir, borderlands studies, folklore, poetry, journalism and children’s literature, often writing about very different versions of Texas.

The best starting list should explain each writer’s Texas connection and suggest an entry point without claiming one book is objectively the author’s “best.”

## 1. Larry McMurtry

McMurtry’s fiction helped shape modern popular images of small-town, urban and frontier Texas. His literary legacy is also physically tied to Archer City, where the Texas Center for the Book helped establish a Literary Landmark at the Archer Public Library.

**Starting point:** *Lonesome Dove* for the epic cattle-drive novel; *The Last Picture Show* for twentieth-century small-town Texas.

## 2. Katherine Anne Porter

Porter’s fiction and nonfiction reached far beyond Texas, but her state roots make her a central Texas literary figure.

Her work is useful for showing that Texas literature developed alongside national and international modernist writing, not outside it.

## 3. O. Henry — William Sydney Porter

O. Henry lived and worked in Austin before becoming famous for short stories known for compressed plots and twist endings.

His Austin connection creates a natural literary-history stop through the O. Henry Museum and downtown cultural district.

## 4. Gloria E. Anzaldúa

Anzaldúa’s work on the U.S.–Mexico borderlands, language, culture and identity transformed how many readers understand South Texas and the border.

TSLAC lists the Dr. Gloria E. Anzaldúa Literary Landmark at the University of Texas Rio Grande Valley Library in Edinburg.

**Starting point:** *Borderlands/La Frontera*.

## 5. Américo Paredes

Paredes combined scholarship, folklore and border literature, making the Rio Grande region central rather than peripheral to Texas cultural history.

His work can link directly to TexasDefined’s Rio Grande Valley and borderlands coverage.

## 6. John Graves

Graves is strongly associated with Texas rivers and landscape writing.

**Starting point:** *Goodbye to a River*, which provides a natural bridge into the Brazos, water history and environmental change.

## 7. J. Frank Dobie

Dobie collected and retold Southwestern folklore and became one of the best-known twentieth-century interpreters of Texas legends.

His work should be read with an understanding that folklore is a record of storytelling and cultural memory, not automatically documented historical fact.

## 8. Dorothy Scarborough

Scarborough wrote fiction, folklore and scholarship connected to Texas and the broader South.

Her work is valuable for readers interested in how regional stories, supernatural traditions and academic study intersected in the early twentieth century.

## 9. Tomás Rivera

Rivera’s writing brought migrant labor, education and Mexican American experience into the center of American literature.

His Texas connections and bilingual literary legacy make him important to a statewide list that does not stop at Anglo frontier writing.

## 10. Bill Wittliff

Writer, screenwriter and photographer Bill Wittliff helped preserve and interpret Southwestern culture across books, television and archives.

His legacy connects literature to the Wittliff Collections at Texas State University and to screen adaptations such as *Lonesome Dove*.

## Literary Texas is visitable

TSLAC’s Literary Landmarks program creates a useful travel layer around writers and reading. Current designated sites include landmarks associated with Larry McMurtry, Gloria Anzaldúa, John A. Lomax and other Texas literary figures.

That lets this page function as both a reading guide and a cultural-road-trip hub.
