# 15 Things to Do on Texas Independence Day — March 2

Texas Independence Day is March 2, the date the Convention of 1836 declared Texas a free and independent republic at Washington-on-the-Brazos. It is easy to reduce the day to flags, slogans and a quick mention of 1836. A better way to mark it is to use the date as an excuse to understand how the Texas Revolution, the Republic era and the people who lived through them actually fit together.

The Texas State Library and Archives Commission preserves the original declaration and explains that the convention began March 1, 1836. George Childress chaired the drafting committee, the declaration was presented the next day, and 59 delegates ultimately approved it. The delegates were working while the siege of the Alamo was still underway.

That makes March 2 a natural day for a Texas history trip, a primary-source lesson, a family outing or a deeper look at the stories that are often compressed into a few famous names.

## 1. Read the Texas Declaration of Independence for yourself

Start with the document, not somebody else's summary of it.

The Texas State Library provides both the original handwritten manuscript and a printed broadside online. Read the declaration closely enough to notice what its writers argued, what political grievances they emphasized and how the language reflects the world of 1836.

For families, compare a paragraph of the Texas declaration with the United States Declaration of Independence. Ask what ideas sound similar and what concerns are specific to Mexican Texas in the 1830s.

## 2. Visit Washington-on-the-Brazos

If one place is naturally associated with March 2, it is Washington-on-the-Brazos State Historic Site.

The Convention of 1836 met there, and the Texas Historical Commission describes the site as the place where the Texas Declaration of Independence was signed. The complex includes the historic townsite, Independence Hall interpretation, the Star of the Republic Museum and Barrington, the home associated with Republic of Texas president Anson Jones.

Do not hard-code a special-event schedule into this article. Independence Day programming, admission details and operating hours should be checked against the site's official calendar immediately before publication and again before travel.

## 3. Put San Felipe de Austin before 1836 on the timeline

Texas independence makes more sense when you understand what came before it.

San Felipe de Austin was the political and commercial center of Stephen F. Austin's colony and later the seat of the provisional government before the Convention of 1836 moved to Washington-on-the-Brazos. The modern state historic site uses museum exhibits, archaeology and reconstructed spaces to explain life in Mexican Texas and the events leading into revolution.

The town was later evacuated and burned during the Runaway Scrape. That story helps connect March 2 to the larger civilian experience of the war rather than treating the declaration as an isolated event.

## 4. Follow the Revolution beyond the Alamo

The Alamo is central to Texas memory, but it is not the whole revolution.

Use March 2 to build a broader sequence: Gonzales, the Consultation, San Felipe, the Convention at Washington, the Alamo, Goliad, the Runaway Scrape and San Jacinto. Once those events are placed on one timeline, the declaration becomes part of a fast-moving military and political crisis rather than a standalone anniversary.

TexasDefined should link this section into deeper Texas Revolution and Republic-era explainers as they are built.

## 5. Visit Goliad and Presidio La Bahía

A Texas Independence Day history trip can include Goliad, where the revolution's story is tied to Presidio La Bahía, the Goliad Campaign and the executions that followed the Battle of Coleto.

This is a good place to resist the temptation to turn every site into a photo stop. Read the interpretation, distinguish the military events from later memory, and give the people involved enough context to be more than names on a monument.

Verify current access, services and programming before publication or travel.

## 6. Walk the San Jacinto Battleground

March 2 marks the declaration of independence; April 21 marks the battlefield victory that secured it militarily.

Visiting the San Jacinto Battleground lets readers connect those two dates. The Texas Historical Commission manages the battleground and describes the Battle of San Jacinto as the decisive battle that resulted in Texas independence from Mexico.

A useful Independence Day article should make that distinction clear: declaring independence and successfully defending it were not the same event.

## 7. Read one delegate's story instead of memorizing all 59 names

The Convention of 1836 included 59 delegates, but a long list of names is not especially memorable.

Choose one signer and trace his route to the convention, community ties, occupation, politics and what happened after independence. Then repeat the exercise with someone from a different region or background.

This turns a list of signatures into a map of the settlements and networks that made up Texas in 1836.

## 8. Learn the Tejano story of the Revolution

Texas independence was not simply a story of Anglo settlers fighting Mexico. Tejanos participated in Texas political life, served in revolutionary forces and experienced the consequences of the conflict in ways that do not fit neatly into a single heroic narrative.

Use March 2 to learn about figures such as José Antonio Navarro, Juan Seguín and other Tejano participants, while also recognizing that loyalties and political goals among Mexican Texans were varied.

A strong Texas history site should make these perspectives part of the main story rather than placing them in a side note.

## 9. Add the Runaway Scrape to the family history lesson

Declarations and battles dominate textbooks because they have clear dates. Civilian flight is harder to summarize.

As Santa Anna's forces advanced, many Texas residents fled east during the Runaway Scrape. San Felipe de Austin's official history describes residents evacuating and the town being burned so it could not be used by the advancing Mexican army.

For younger readers, this is an effective way to ask what war looked like for families who were not standing in a fort or signing a document.

## 10. Build a six-week Texas Revolution calendar

Instead of studying March 2 alone, map the revolution from late February through April 21.

Mark the Alamo siege, March 2 declaration, fall of the Alamo, Goliad, the Runaway Scrape and San Jacinto. Add where Sam Houston's army moved and where the government relocated.

The result shows how quickly the defining events unfolded. It also creates an annual TexasDefined content series that can link multiple history pages together every spring.

## 11. Use the State Archives as a primary-source rabbit hole

The Texas State Library and Archives Commission has digitized the declaration and other Republic-era records. Spend an hour following original documents instead of reading recycled trivia.

Look at handwriting, names, endorsements, dates and document formats. Ask what the archivists know for certain, what is inferred and what stories have changed through later retelling.

This is also an ideal place to teach children the difference between a primary source and a modern summary.

## 12. Fly the Texas flag — and learn what it actually represents

Putting out a Texas flag on March 2 is easy. Make the gesture more meaningful by learning when the familiar Lone Star flag was adopted and how it fits into the Republic period rather than assuming every Texas Revolution image used today's flag.

This can cross-link to TexasDefined's Texas flag explainer and any future flag-history page.

Avoid repeating viral flag-etiquette claims unless they are checked against current Texas and federal flag guidance.

## 13. Cook a Texas meal, but do not pretend it is an 1836 reenactment

Food is one of the easiest ways to turn an anniversary into a family tradition. Barbecue, chili, beans, cornbread, smoked meat, kolaches or another Texas favorite can all work.

Just label it honestly as a modern Texas celebration unless the recipes are actually researched from period sources. Texas food culture developed across Indigenous, Mexican, Tejano, German, Czech, African American, Southern and other traditions over generations.

Use the meal to link into TexasDefined's deeper food and barbecue coverage instead of inventing a fake "official Independence Day menu."

## 14. Visit the history closest to home

Not everyone can drive to Washington County on March 2.

Use the day to visit a county museum, courthouse square, local historical marker, archive or historic cemetery. Search for people from your county who participated in the revolution, Republic government, migration routes or early statehood.

This is where TexasDefined's county pages can become much more useful: statewide anniversaries should push readers toward local history rather than keeping every story centered on Austin, San Antonio or Houston.

## 15. Plan a Texas Revolution road trip for later in the spring

March 2 does not have to be the day you visit every historic site. Use it to plan a route for March or April.

A practical history circuit could connect San Felipe de Austin, Washington-on-the-Brazos, San Antonio, Goliad and San Jacinto, with additional stops chosen around the traveler's starting point. That route follows political, military and civilian parts of the story instead of treating Texas history as a collection of unrelated landmarks.

Verify operating hours, construction, ticketing and special-event schedules at every managed site before travel.

## What Texas Independence Day actually commemorates

March 2 commemorates the 1836 declaration at Washington-on-the-Brazos. It does not mark the Battle of San Jacinto, Texas statehood or the adoption of every later symbol associated with Texas.

Keeping those dates separate makes the story more interesting:

- **March 2, 1836:** independence declared at Washington-on-the-Brazos.
- **March 6, 1836:** the Alamo fell after the siege.
- **March 27, 1836:** executions at Goliad followed the earlier surrender after Coleto.
- **April 21, 1836:** Texian forces defeated Santa Anna's army at San Jacinto.
- **December 29, 1845:** Texas was admitted to the United States.
- **February 19, 1846:** the Republic-era government formally transferred authority to the state government.

That timeline gives TexasDefined several natural internal-link opportunities instead of forcing one article to carry the entire revolution.
