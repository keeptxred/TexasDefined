# 12 Invasive Species Causing Trouble in Texas Ecosystems

“Invasive” is not a synonym for foreign, ugly or unwanted. In ecology, the useful distinction is whether a nonnative organism establishes, spreads and causes meaningful ecological, economic or human-health harm.

Texas also has multiple regulatory lists. TexasInvasives.org explicitly warns that its recommended plant list is not itself a legal prohibition list; legal restrictions can come from the Texas Department of Agriculture, Texas Parks and Wildlife Department or other authorities. This article should keep that distinction visible.

## 1. Feral hogs

Feral hogs disturb soil, damage crops, compete with native wildlife and degrade wetlands and streambanks. Their impacts span agriculture, wildlife habitat and water quality.

Because control methods are regulated and situation-specific, TexasDefined should explain the problem without turning this page into a DIY trapping or hunting manual.

## 2. Zebra mussels

Zebra mussels attach to hard surfaces in huge numbers, foul water infrastructure and alter lake ecosystems by filtering plankton from the water column.

Their spread is also why Texas boaters see repeated “clean, drain and dry” messaging. Current infested-lake status must be checked before publication because the distribution changes.

## 3. Giant salvinia

Giant salvinia is a floating aquatic fern capable of forming dense mats that block light, reduce oxygen and interfere with boating and fishing.

It is a strong example of why moving boats, trailers or aquatic plants between water bodies can spread an invasive species without anyone intending to do so.

## 4. Hydrilla

Hydrilla can form dense underwater vegetation in lakes and reservoirs. It can impede recreation and alter habitat, although aquatic-plant management can be complicated because fish and wildlife may use the structure once it is established.

Avoid simplistic “all vegetation is bad” framing.

## 5. Chinese tallow

Chinese tallow spreads aggressively in East Texas, coastal prairie and disturbed landscapes, replacing diverse native plant communities with dense stands.

The species is especially relevant to Houston/Gulf Coast county pages because it can transform prairie and woodland edges.

## 6. Giant reed

Giant reed, or Arundo donax, invades riparian corridors and can form tall dense stands along waterways. It alters habitat and can complicate water-management and fire issues.

TexasInvasives.org and Texas agriculture resources should be used to confirm current regulatory status before promotion.

## 7. Saltcedar

Saltcedar/tamarisk has invaded river corridors in arid parts of Texas and the Southwest. Its impacts and management history are complex, involving water use, sediment, native vegetation and wildlife habitat.

The article should avoid outdated one-cause explanations and use current agency ecology sources.

## 8. Emerald ash borer

Emerald ash borer is an invasive insect that kills ash trees. Its Texas distribution has expanded over time, making county-level current-status checks essential.

This topic can cross-link to the Texas trees guide and future urban-forestry pages.

## 9. Red imported fire ant

Red imported fire ants affect agriculture, wildlife, pets and people across much of Texas. They can displace native ant communities and are especially visible in disturbed lawns and pastures.

Management advice should rely on Texas A&M AgriLife rather than folk remedies.

## 10. Apple snails

Large nonnative apple snails occur in Texas aquatic systems and can damage vegetation and alter wetland food webs.

TexasInvasives.org maintains animal records for invasive snails and other aquatic organisms; current distribution should be checked before naming affected counties.

## 11. Armored catfish

Nonnative armored catfishes have established populations in some Texas waterways. Burrowing behavior can contribute to bank erosion, while dense populations may alter aquatic communities.

This is a good example of aquarium-release problems and the rule that unwanted pets should never be released into natural water.

## 12. Invasive tilapia and other prohibited/exotic fish

Several nonnative fish have established Texas populations or are regulated because of ecological risk. “Tilapia” is not one regulatory category, so species-specific TPWD rules matter.

Before publication, verify the exact legal status of each fish rather than copying a generic invasive list.

## Not every nonnative species is legally prohibited

TexasInvasives.org explicitly distinguishes its educational/recommended database from state legal lists. A species can be ecologically concerning without being illegal to possess, while another species may be specifically regulated by TDA or TPWD.

That distinction should appear near the top of the published page, not hidden in fine print.

## What ordinary Texans can do

- Never release aquarium fish, plants, bait or pets into the wild.
- Clean, drain and dry boats and gear between water bodies.
- Use native or well-behaved landscape plants where practical.
- Learn current county quarantine or pest rules before moving firewood or plant material.
- Report suspicious invasive observations through recognized state/citizen-science channels.
- Follow agency guidance rather than improvising pesticide, poison or wildlife-control methods.
