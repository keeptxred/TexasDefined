# 12 Things to Know About Texas Water Rights and Laws

Texas water law becomes much easier to understand once you stop treating “water” as one legal category. Surface water and groundwater operate under different legal frameworks, local groundwater districts can add another layer, and an apparently simple pond, creek or well can raise questions that depend on the exact source, location and use.

This is a practical overview, not legal advice. Anyone buying land, planning a major withdrawal, transferring a water right, drilling a commercial well or relying on water for a business should verify the current law and local rules with the relevant agency, district and qualified counsel.

## 1. Surface water and groundwater are legally different systems

Texas does not administer river water and groundwater through one universal permit system.

TCEQ defines “state water” to include water in rivers, natural streams, lakes, bays and other specified watercourses. TCEQ also expressly notes that state water does not include percolating groundwater or diffuse rainfall runoff before it reaches a watercourse.

That first distinction controls much of what follows.

## 2. Texas owns surface water; users receive rights to use it

TCEQ explains that surface water in Texas is owned by the state and held in trust for the citizens of Texas. The state grants rights to use that water for municipal, agricultural, industrial and other authorized purposes.

A water right is therefore not the same thing as owning the river itself.

## 3. Most diversions of state surface water require authorization

A person generally needs state authorization to divert, store or use state surface water unless a specific statutory exemption applies.

Common exemptions include certain domestic and livestock uses, wildlife-management uses and specified emergency uses. The details matter, so a landowner should not assume that a small diversion is exempt merely because it occurs on private property.

## 4. Surface-water rights have priority dates

Texas surface-water rights generally follow the prior-appropriation principle summarized as “first in time, first in right.” A right with an earlier priority date is senior to a right with a later priority date.

That priority can matter enormously during shortage conditions.

## 5. Drought can force junior water rights to curtail

TCEQ explains that when curtailment becomes necessary, junior permitted rights may have to reduce or stop diversions so senior rights can be satisfied.

This is why the face amount of a water right should never be confused with a guarantee that the same quantity will be physically available every year.

## 6. Watermaster areas add active administration

Texas has watermaster programs in portions of the state where water-right administration is handled more actively. TCEQ identifies the Brazos, Concho, Rio Grande and South Texas watermaster areas.

Watermaster staff track diversions, available supply and priority administration. The exact rules differ by program; the Lower and Middle Rio Grande, for example, use an account-based allocation system tied to Falcon-Amistad storage rather than simply mirroring every other basin.

## 7. Water rights can carry transaction and amendment issues

A surface-water authorization can involve a specific owner, diversion point, place of use, purpose of use, amount, priority date and other permit terms. Changes can require TCEQ review or amendment.

That makes water-right due diligence important in land transactions. A seller saying “the property has water rights” is only the beginning of the research. Confirm the actual authorization, ownership status, amount, priority, diversion location, use restrictions and any pending amendments or enforcement issues.

## 8. Groundwater is not governed by the same TCEQ surface-water system

Percolating groundwater is excluded from TCEQ's definition of state surface water. Texas groundwater law therefore starts from a different legal framework.

The Texas Water Development Board describes groundwater as property of the landowner, historically governed by the rule of capture, while also emphasizing that the rule is limited by court decisions and by groundwater-district regulation.

## 9. The rule of capture does not mean “pump anything you want”

The shorthand version of Texas groundwater law is often repeated as if a landowner has an unlimited right to pump. That is misleading.

TWDB notes recognized limits involving malicious pumping intended to harm a neighbor, wasteful pumping, negligent pumping that causes subsidence on adjoining land and slant wells crossing property lines. More importantly for many current landowners, groundwater conservation districts may impose permitting, spacing and production rules.

Treat the rule of capture as a starting principle, not a complete operating manual.

## 10. Groundwater conservation districts can regulate wells and production

TWDB says groundwater conservation districts are local governmental entities authorized to regulate well spacing, groundwater production or both. District rules can include permits for non-exempt wells and other measures intended to conserve, protect and manage groundwater.

As of TWDB's current information, Texas has 98 confirmed groundwater conservation districts covering nearly 70 percent of the state's area. Those numbers are date-sensitive and should be refreshed before publication.

The practical rule is simple: check whether the exact property lies inside a GCD, then read that district's current rules rather than relying on a statewide summary.

## 11. Some regions have special groundwater authorities and subsidence rules

Not every groundwater-regulation area fits neatly into the ordinary GCD model. Special authorities can impose their own permitting and withdrawal regimes.

The Edwards Aquifer Authority, for example, administers groundwater permits and well-construction requirements within its jurisdiction. In the Houston-Galveston region, subsidence districts regulate groundwater withdrawals to address land subsidence.

This is another reason a statewide “you own the groundwater, so drill a well” answer is not sufficient.

## 12. The exact parcel, source and proposed use control the real answer

Before relying on water connected to a Texas property, identify:

- whether the source is surface water, groundwater, captured rainwater or another category;
- whether a TCEQ surface-water right exists and what it actually authorizes;
- whether the property lies in a groundwater conservation district, subsidence district or special authority;
- whether the well is exempt, registered, permitted or subject to spacing/production rules;
- whether drought restrictions or priority administration can affect supply;
- whether a proposed change in ownership, diversion point, purpose or place of use needs approval;
- whether deed language, easements, contracts or reservations affect access to the water.

Water rights are one of the areas where the exact address and exact intended use matter more than a generic statewide answer.

## Rainwater and runoff are another reason definitions matter

TCEQ distinguishes diffuse surface rainfall runoff from state water before that runoff reaches a defined watercourse. Once water reaches a watercourse or otherwise falls within the statutory definition of state water, different rules may apply.

Do not use that distinction to improvise a dam, diversion or impoundment project. Check TCEQ requirements before altering drainage or capturing water connected to a natural stream.

## A practical land-buyer checklist

For rural or acreage property, ask these questions before closing:

1. What is the current water source for the property?
2. If there is a well, what aquifer does it draw from and is a State of Texas Well Report available?
3. Is the parcel inside a GCD or special groundwater authority?
4. What current district rules apply to the existing well and any future well?
5. If the seller claims surface-water rights, what is the TCEQ water-right number and current ownership record?
6. What is the surface-water priority date and authorized use?
7. Are there drought, watermaster or production restrictions that can reduce practical availability?
8. Does the purchase contract actually transfer the relevant water rights, permits, equipment and easements?
