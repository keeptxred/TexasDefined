# 15 Kinds of Handmade Texas Goods Worth Seeking Out

A useful “made in Texas” guide should do more than slap a Lone Star on imported merchandise. The stronger angle is craftsmanship: goods actually designed, made, grown or substantially produced by Texas makers, with enough information for readers to understand the regional tradition behind them.

This page should be category-led rather than a permanent list of sellers. Individual businesses can be added only after current operating and origin claims are verified.

## 1. Custom and small-batch cowboy boots

Texas has a deep bootmaking tradition, especially in El Paso, Fort Worth, Houston and other Western-wear centers. Handmade/custom should be distinguished from factory-made, made-to-order and merely Texas-branded boots.

Link to the dedicated custom-boot article rather than turning this page into a maker ranking.

## 2. Hand-shaped felt and straw hats

A properly shaped Western hat can reflect the wearer’s preference in crown, brim and fit. Some Texas hat shops shape factory-made blanks; others produce more of the hat themselves.

The final article should be precise about what “handmade” means for each maker.

## 3. Tooled leather goods

Belts, wallets, saddlebags, tack and bags can showcase hand tooling, stamping, carving and finishing. Leather origin and final assembly location should be verified separately.

## 4. Saddles and tack

Working saddles connect craft directly to ranching. Fit, tree construction, rigging and intended riding use matter more than decorative appearance.

Avoid presenting safety-critical tack as a souvenir purchase without qualified fitting.

## 5. Texas pottery

Regional pottery traditions include functional tableware, decorative ceramics and studio work using Texas imagery or local clays/glazes where documented.

A future local-market database could link pottery studios to county/place pages.

## 6. Quilts and textile work

Quilting guilds, family traditions and contemporary fiber artists preserve a practical form of folk art. A handmade quilt is fundamentally different from a Texas-print mass-produced blanket.

## 7. Silversmithing and Western jewelry

Handmade buckles, conchos, rings and other silverwork connect ranching, rodeo and Western design traditions. Metal purity and stone origin should not be assumed from appearance.

## 8. Blacksmith and metalwork

Fireplace tools, architectural hardware, knives where lawful, ranch hardware and decorative ironwork can all come from Texas smiths.

The published shopping guide should exclude regulated weapons and focus on household/artisan metalwork.

## 9. Woodworking and furniture

Texas cabinetmakers and furniture builders work in styles ranging from Hill Country rustic to contemporary studio furniture. Local wood claims should be verified if highlighted.

## 10. Hand-painted signs and folk art

Painted signs, murals, carved signs and outsider/visionary art fit naturally with the existing Texas architecture and roadside-art cluster.

## 11. Woven goods and baskets

Basketry, weaving and other fiber crafts can have Indigenous, Mexican-American, rural and contemporary studio-art roots. Cultural attribution should be specific rather than generic “Southwestern.”

## 12. Handmade soaps and body products

Small-batch soaps and personal-care products are common at Texas markets. Avoid unsupported health or therapeutic claims; focus on ingredients, process and maker transparency.

## 13. Candles and home fragrance

This is an easy category for local makers but also easy for resellers. Verify that the business actually pours/makes the products it markets as handmade.

## 14. Texas food products made by small producers

Salsa, preserves, pecan products, hot sauce, honey and baked goods can be strong regional gifts. Food-safety, cottage-food and shipping rules vary by product and seller.

Do not confuse “Texas recipe” with “made in Texas.”

## 15. Printmaking, photography and local art

A print or photograph of an exact Texas subject can be a more authentic souvenir than generic branded merchandise, provided the artist owns the work and reproduction rights.

## How TexasDefined should verify a maker

Before naming a seller, check:

- current Texas business/location;
- where the product is actually made;
- whether the seller is the maker or a reseller;
- materials/process claims;
- current ordering/shipping status;
- return/custom-order policy;
- image/reproduction permissions.

## Source / verification notes

Use maker websites and current Texas market/guild sources for named businesses. Texas Comptroller guidance applies to taxable sales at fairs/markets and online commerce where relevant: https://comptroller.texas.gov/taxes/publications/96-211.php

Do not claim a product is “Texas-made” solely because the company is headquartered in Texas.

## Image guidance

Use an exact Texas maker at work—boot stitching, leather tooling, pottery, silverwork or woodworking—with permission and rights verified. Avoid generic craft-table stock photography.
