# 10 Facts About the Texas Interstate Highway System

Texas's interstate network looks simple on a road-trip map until you notice the split routes, three-digit loops, cross-state freight corridors and several future interstates being assembled from existing highways. The system is both old infrastructure and an active construction/planning project.

This article should explain how the network works rather than chase a brittle “Texas has exactly X miles” statistic. Current corridor designations and signed mileage must be rechecked before publication.

## 1. Odd and even interstate numbers tell you the general direction

TxDOT explains that odd-numbered interstate routes generally run north-south, while even-numbered routes generally run east-west.

That is why I-35 is a north-south spine and I-10/I-20/I-40 cross the state east-west.

## 2. The original numbering system used 5s and 0s as major anchors

The original national system generally assigned north-south routes numbers ending in 5 and east-west routes numbers ending in 0. Later routes filled the numerical gaps with other odd and even numbers.

TxDOT uses I-27 as an example: its number falls geographically between I-25 and I-35.

## 3. Texas has unusual split routes in major metro areas

I-35 divides into I-35E and I-35W around Dallas-Fort Worth before rejoining. The split is a reminder that interstate numbering is not always one continuous shield on one roadway.

A future TexasDefined road-trip page should explain metro-specific routing instead of telling readers simply to “stay on I-35.”

## 4. Three-digit interstate numbers usually connect to a parent route

TxDOT says interstate loops use three digits and are associated with a primary interstate route. Texas examples include I-410 in San Antonio, I-610 in Houston, I-635 in Dallas and I-820 in Fort Worth.

The exact function can vary—loop, bypass or spur—but the numbering is not random.

## 5. The Interstate System is part of a larger National Highway System

The National Highway System includes the interstates plus other principal arterials and connectors serving ports, airports, transit facilities and strategic military needs.

That is why a Texas transportation article should distinguish “interstate highway” from “important highway.” Many major Texas freight and travel corridors carry U.S. or state highway numbers instead.

## 6. Texas is still building new interstate systems

The network is not frozen in the 1950s. TxDOT is developing several future interstate corridors by upgrading existing highways to interstate standards over time.

That process is incremental: a corridor can be federally authorized or designated for future interstate development long before every mile carries an interstate shield.

## 7. I-27 is expanding far beyond its familiar Panhandle footprint

TxDOT's current I-27 System plan describes a 963-mile Texas corridor connecting West and South Texas to border gateways and north toward the international trade route to Canada.

As of the current 2026 TxDOT page, about 124 miles are signed as I-27 from Amarillo through Lubbock-area routing. Treat that mileage as date-sensitive.

## 8. I-14 is planned as a major Central Texas and Gulf-oriented system

TxDOT says the planned I-14 System in Texas is projected to exceed 1,000 miles and incorporates portions of multiple existing highways.

Only a small portion is currently signed as I-14. The rest is a long-term corridor concept requiring planning, environmental work, design, funding and construction before designation.

## 9. I-69 is also being built through incremental designations

Texas's I-69 system uses portions of highways including US 59, US 77 and US 281, with separate interstate designations appearing as segments meet standards.

TxDOT says more than 172 miles had been designated and signed when its current page was published. Recheck before publication because that figure can grow.

## 10. Texas also maintains a rural Highway Trunk System

The Texas Highway Trunk System is a network of rural divided highways designed to complement the rural Interstate System and connect population centers, ports and major entry points.

TxDOT limits the Trunk System to 11,500 miles. That system is one reason long-distance Texas travel depends on more than roads carrying an “I-” shield.

## Why this matters to travelers

For everyday driving, the practical lessons are simple:

- Do not assume a future interstate designation means a freeway is complete today.
- Expect major construction as corridors are upgraded in pieces.
- Use exact route segments when writing road-trip directions.
- Distinguish interstate routes from tollways, U.S. highways, state highways and farm-to-market roads.
- Recheck current TxDOT project maps before turning planning language into travel advice.
