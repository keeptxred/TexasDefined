# Texas Ghost Towns and Vanished Communities Worth Knowing

Texas ghost towns are not one thing. Some are abandoned mining camps, some are former ports destroyed by storms, some are railroad settlements bypassed by later transportation, and some are communities that faded after a county seat, water source or industry moved elsewhere.

This article should not encourage trespassing or “ruin hunting.” The history matters even when little remains to see.

## Terlingua — Brewster County

Terlingua grew around mercury mining and the Chisos Mining Company. Texas Historical Commission materials describe a once-busy company town that declined after quicksilver markets collapsed and mining ended in the 1940s.

Today it is better described as a historic ghost-town district with residents, businesses and tourism than as an abandoned town. The cemetery, ruins and former company-town landscape remain important historical features.

## Shafter — Presidio County

Shafter grew around silver mining near the Chinati Mountains. Texas Historical Commission travel material identifies it as a ghost town along Highway 67 north of Presidio, with historic cemeteries and surviving structures.

Current road access and private-property boundaries must be verified before any visitor guidance is published.

## Thurber — Erath County

Thurber was a major coal-and-brick company town tied to the Texas and Pacific system. As locomotives shifted away from coal and the regional economy changed, the town declined; THC describes it as a ghost town by the late 1930s.

The W.K. Gordon Center and preserved structures make Thurber one of the best places to understand a vanished industrial community without wandering through unsafe ruins.

## Indianola — Calhoun County

Indianola was once a major Gulf port and immigration gateway. Hurricanes and repeated destruction ultimately ended the city’s role as a major settlement.

THC historical markers document the site and cemetery. This entry should connect ghost-town history to Gulf storms, ports, German immigration and coastal change.

## Stiles — Reagan County

Stiles was an early Reagan County seat before Big Lake became the center of county government. The former courthouse ruins remain one of the visual reminders of the settlement.

Access should be verified because ruins and surrounding land may not function like a conventional public attraction.

## Shafter Lake — Andrews County

THC’s marker describes Shafter Lake as an early townsite in what became Andrews County. It lost out to Andrews in the county-seat vote and faded as activity shifted.

The site is useful because it shows how a single civic decision could determine the future of an early Plains settlement.

## Penwell and other Ector County boom communities

A Texas Historical Commission marker on Ector County’s vanished communities describes settlements that developed around railroads, water sources and oil fields, then diminished as economic activity shifted.

Penwell, Douro, Judkins, Metz, Prairie Home and others can be treated together as a case study in how boomtown geography changed around Odessa.

## Why Texas has so many vanished communities

The recurring causes are more interesting than a ruin count:

- railroads moved or bypassed settlements;
- county seats shifted;
- mines closed;
- oil booms ended;
- water sources failed or drought intensified;
- highways replaced older routes;
- hurricanes destroyed coastal settlements;
- schools, churches and post offices consolidated elsewhere.

## Ghost-town etiquette matters

A “ghost town” label does not mean land is public. Some historic townsites are on private property, contain active residences, include cemeteries or sit beside dangerous structures and active transportation corridors.

Before publication, every entry should be assigned one of these access labels:

- public historic site;
- public roadside marker/viewpoint;
- private property / view only from public right-of-way;
- access uncertain — research before travel;
- active community with ghost-town history.

That is more useful than telling readers to “explore abandoned buildings.”
