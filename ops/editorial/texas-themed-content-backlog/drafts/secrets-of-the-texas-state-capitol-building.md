# 15 Texas State Capitol Facts That Are Better Than the Myths

The Texas State Capitol has enough real history that it does not need invented underground-tunnel stories or recycled trivia. Its granite, financing, construction, art, restoration and even the statue on the dome all have well-documented stories.

This article should explicitly separate verified Capitol facts from legends.

## 1. The current Capitol replaced a building that burned in 1881

The earlier limestone Capitol on the site burned on November 9, 1881. Plans for the new building reportedly escaped the fire.

## 2. Elijah E. Myers won the design competition

State officials selected Detroit architect Elijah E. Myers after a nationwide competition for the new Capitol design.

## 3. Texas Sunset Red granite changed the building’s appearance

Construction began with local limestone, but discoloration problems helped lead builders to use granite from Granite Mountain in Burnet County. The granite owners donated the stone required for the exterior.

## 4. A railroad was built to move the granite

State Preservation Board history says workers shipped 188,518 cubic feet of granite from the quarry on a specially constructed railroad.

That makes the Capitol a useful link between architecture, rail history and Central Texas geology.

## 5. The cornerstone was laid on Texas Independence Day

The 12,000-pound cornerstone was laid on March 2, 1885—Texas Independence Day.

## 6. More than 1,000 people worked on the project

The State Preservation Board says more than 1,000 people helped build the Capitol. Its construction also involved labor disputes and imported stonecutters, a reminder that monumental architecture is labor history as well as political history.

## 7. The building was dedicated in 1888

Major dedication events took place in May 1888, drawing large crowds to Austin.

## 8. The Goddess of Liberty was assembled from dozens of zinc pieces

The original Goddess of Liberty was fabricated from roughly 80 zinc pieces welded into major sections and then hoisted to the top of the dome.

## 9. The statue on the dome today is a replica

The original Goddess deteriorated after decades of weather exposure. It was removed in the 1980s, restored and placed in a controlled indoor setting. A lighter aluminum replica now crowns the Capitol.

## 10. A time capsule was found in the original Goddess

Preservation work revealed a time capsule in the star held by the original statue. The contents included period documents and printed materials.

## 11. The Texas Capitol really is taller than the U.S. Capitol by one common measurement

The State Preservation Board’s myths-and-legends page gives the Texas Capitol’s height as 302.64 feet from the south-front ground level to the tip of the Goddess’s star, compared with 288 feet for the U.S. Capitol from its east-front ground level to the top of the Statue of Freedom.

Do not turn that into a vague “everything is bigger” claim; state the measurement basis.

## 12. The famous Rotunda-floor crack story is false

One legend claims a crack in the Rotunda floor came from a fatal fall. State Preservation Board research rejects that story: painter Ed Wheeler died in a fall in 1922, but the current terrazzo floor was not installed until 1936.

This is a good example of why the article should distinguish folklore from documentation.

## 13. The Capitol contains a major underground extension

The 1990s preservation project added a large underground extension north of the historic building to relieve overcrowding while preserving the Capitol’s exterior character.

Current public-access details should be verified before turning this into visitor guidance.

## 14. The 1990s restoration reversed decades of alterations

By the late twentieth century, overcrowding, mechanical systems and piecemeal remodeling had changed much of the building. The preservation project restored many spaces toward their late nineteenth- and early twentieth-century appearance while upgrading safety and building systems.

## 15. The Visitors Center explains the Capitol’s construction better than most trivia lists

Current exhibits include a dome model, construction materials, the original Goddess star, Capitol timelines and the story of the land deal that helped finance construction.

## Myths worth labeling as myths

The State Preservation Board maintains a dedicated Capitol myths-and-legends resource. That is useful editorially because it lets TexasDefined answer popular questions without presenting folklore as fact.

Examples include:

- buried Confederate gold on the Capitol grounds;
- the Rotunda-floor crack legend;
- exaggerated or context-free size claims.
